THE BURIED WORLD
Episode 16 - Solved Every Few Years. Read By Nobody: The Voynich Manuscript

How this film was made, and what was corrected before recording

Last updated 5 November 2026


WHY THIS FILE EXISTS

Every episode of this channel is checked line by line against the publications
before it is recorded, and the corrections are published. They are the most
useful thing here: they show where the ordinary version of a story is wrong,
and they show what this film got wrong first.

For the Voynich manuscript the checking was unusually strict, because the film
is about claims. A film that says "none of these readings passed the test" has
to pass its own. Nine statements in the first draft were cut or rewritten. The
table at the end lists every statement of fact in the film with its source and
the date it was checked.


1. "ABOUT 240 PAGES" IS NOT WHAT THE LIBRARY SAYS

The figure of about 240 pages is everywhere, including in the 2009 materials
report and in the press coverage of the radiocarbon date. It is not in the
Beinecke Library's own catalogue, which gives the extent as "ff. 102 +1", that
is one hundred and two leaves plus one, several of them folding out into
multiple panels. The film uses the catalogue's count and says that some leaves
fold out. The two figures are not in conflict - they count different things -
but a film about precision uses the catalogued one.


2. THE RADIOCARBON RANGE IS GIVEN WITHOUT A CONFIDENCE FIGURE

The parchment was dated at the University of Arizona in 2009 and the result,
1404-1438, was announced in February 2011. The first draft added "with
ninety-five per cent confidence". The underlying report is not public; the
confidence figure appears only in a secondary recalculation, which also arrives
at a slightly different upper bound (1435). The film gives the range as
announced and nothing more. It also says plainly what the date is a date of:
the animal skins, not the writing on them.


3. THE EMPEROR'S PURCHASE IS HEARSAY, AND THE FILM SAYS SO

The story that Rudolf II paid six hundred ducats for the book comes from one
letter, written in Prague in 1665 or 1666 (the library itself gives both years)
and addressed to Athanasius Kircher. The writer reports what he had been told
by a third person. The library's own summary page states the purchase as a
fact; the library's transcription of the letter shows it is not. The film calls
it "a story the writer had been told".

What the film gives instead as hard evidence is the faded signature on the
first page, legible under ultraviolet light and in the 2024 multispectral
images: the name of a man who served at Rudolf's court and died in 1622.


4. "NOT A SINGLE CONFIRMED WORD" WAS CUT

It is a good line and the sources do not support it. Several short stretches of
the book are in ordinary letters and can be read: the month names on the
zodiac pages, the lines on the last page, the alphabet columns on the first.
What the sources do support is weaker and more precise: the Beinecke says that
"cryptographic approaches have failed to decipher the text, if it is indeed
encoded", and the 2021 review in the Annual Review of Linguistics says that
"there is at present no consensus on the language underlying the manuscript".
The film says nobody has produced a reading that anybody else accepts.


5. THE WORD-REPETITION CLAIM WAS WRONG AND WAS REPLACED

The first draft said that words repeat side by side "far more often than any
language allows". The 2021 review measured it: the rate of a word being
repeated immediately is 0.84 per cent in one dialect and 0.94 per cent in the
other, which the authors describe as "within the realm of plausibility for
natural language texts". The claim was removed. The anomalies the film does
describe are the ones the review supports: words sitting beside near-copies of
themselves that differ by a single sign, the line behaving as a unit, and a
conditional entropy lower than in any of the 250 languages compared.


6. THE AI VIDEO IS DESCRIBED AS IT IS, NOT AS ITS TITLE SOUNDS

The video that prompted this episode - a million views in six weeks - is
titled as if an artificial intelligence had found something nobody expected.
Watched to the end, it does something more modest and more honest: it hands the
text to a commercial AI and asks it to measure rather than translate, and it
ends in what it calls a verdict of ambiguity. Its own description says that
nothing in it claims the manuscript has revealed its secret. The film reports
that, and credits it. The one claim in the video the sources contradict - that
no language repeats words the way this text does - is not repeated here.


7. DATES THAT NEEDED CARE

- The Bristol announcement of May 2019 was published on 15 May and removed
  within days; the university's statement is dated 16 May and other reports
  give 17 May. The film says "within days".
- The 2019 paper carries a print date of January 2019 and an online date of
  29 April 2019; the film says "2019".
- The 2020 paper on the generating algorithm appeared online in May 2019 and
  in a 2020 issue; the film says "2020", the issue year.
- The Pylos tablet that confirmed Linear B was excavated in June 1952, the same
  summer as the decipherment was announced, and read by its excavator in the
  spring of 1953. An earlier draft said "the next year"; corrected.


8. NAMES THAT ARE NOT SPOKEN

The researchers behind the 1921, 2014, 2017 and 2019 readings are not named in
the narration. They are named here and in the source list. The film's argument
is about a pattern - a reading that stops where somebody else would have to
use it - and not about any individual, two of whom are living. The quotations
from the University of Bristol's statement are given in the statement's own
words.


HOW THE PICTURES WERE MADE

Twenty-four of the fifty-six shots show the manuscript itself. These are not
generated. They are the Beinecke Library's own high-resolution scans of MS 408,
taken from the library's IIIF image service, shown whole or cropped and
otherwise unaltered; a list of every file with its source address is kept with
the production. Credit: Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, Yale
University.

The legal basis for using them is stated here because the channel's own
guidelines flag photographs of public-domain objects as a risk under German
law: (1) the manuscript is an anonymous work of the fifteenth century and in
the public domain; (2) since June 2021, section 68 of the German Copyright Act
provides that reproductions of public-domain visual works are not protected by
related rights, so the photographer's right that would otherwise attach to a
reproduction does not apply; (3) the Beinecke's copyright page states that
"Yale University claims no rights in public domain works", and Yale's 2011
open-access policy makes images of works believed to be in the public domain
available without restriction. The library's object page carries a general
notice that use "may be subject to the copyright law of the United States" and
does not itself declare the scans public domain; the film therefore does not
say the library "released" them, only that it put them online for study, which
is what its own description says.

The remaining twenty photograph-like shots - offices, laboratories, a reading
room, a bookshop, four painted historical scenes - are generated, and the EU
marking is on screen in the first fifteen seconds and on every such shot. No
generated image shows a page of the manuscript, and no generated image contains
writing of any kind: in a film about a script nobody can read, invented script
would be a falsehood. No generated image shows the face of any real person.

The twelve diagrams are drawn in code. Where they show measurements that were
not made for this film - the word-frequency curve, the entropy bars, the
distribution of the two dialects - they say "schematic" in their own footers.
Where three possibilities are set side by side, each gets the same area, the
same type size and the same colour. The film does not decide between them, so
the graphics are not allowed to decide either.
