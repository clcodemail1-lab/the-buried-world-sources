THE BURIED WORLD
Dispatch 09 - Nothing Here Happened All At Once
Full source list

Four papers, all in the Journal of Archaeological Science, carried by its
September and October 2026 issues. They come from different fields and
different continents, and they are together in one film because each of them
takes the same habit apart: reading an archaeological pattern as one
simultaneous event.

🚨 A NOTE ON THOSE DATES, because it is the kind of thing this film is about.
September and October are ISSUE dates, not publication days. Elsevier assigns a
journal issue months ahead, and an article appears online long before the issue
it is bound into. Crossref records only `published-print` for all four of these
- there is no online date in the record at all - and the Crossref entries
themselves were created on 30 July, 3 August, 25 August and 27 August 2026.
That is the deposit date of the DOI, and for this publisher it sits at or very
near the day the article went online - it is the closest thing to a publication
day these records contain, and it is offered as that and not as more.

An earlier version of this list, and of the film's commentary, said the papers
were "published in September or October". That was wrong in exactly the way the
film warns against: a date field was read as an event when it was a label. The
commentary now names the issues as issues.

Every DOI below was checked against the Crossref record - title, journal,
volume, pages and author list, not merely clicked. That check is not decorum:
in a previous dispatch a DOI that resolved perfectly turned out to belong to a
different paper.

WHAT THIS LIST HAS TO SAY ABOUT ITSELF, and it belongs at the top rather than
in a footnote: the full texts of all four papers were not machine-readable from
here. ScienceDirect refuses automated requests, and two repository copies sit
behind bot checks. Every figure quoted in this film therefore comes from the
authors' own abstract, plus background that is marked as background where it
appears. Nothing in the film goes beyond what those abstracts state. Where a
number would have needed the full text - how many times a piece of glass was
remelted, the date of the burnt building, the name of the villa - the film says
so instead of estimating.

ONE THING THIS FILM DOES NOT SHOW: human remains. Not a bone, not a skull, not
a tooth - in any shot, including the section on cannibalism. That section is
carried by a drawn diagram and by pictures of places and instruments. The
reason is not squeamishness: the finding being described is a chronology, and a
picture of butchered bone would pull the attention exactly where the text is
not looking.

ITEM 1 - THE BURNT BUILDING THAT WAS FILLED TWICE

  Klinkenberg, V., Gavryushkina, M. and Hadjigavriel, M. (2026).
  Unpacking the burnt building: 3D spatial analysis challenges single-event
  interpretations of prehistoric house conflagration.
  Journal of Archaeological Science 193, 106660. September 2026 issue;
  Crossref record created 30 July 2026.
  doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2026.106660

  WHAT WAS DONE: at Chlorakas-Palloures, a Chalcolithic settlement on the west
  coast of Cyprus north of Paphos, a building that burned while filled with
  stacks of ceramic and stone artefacts was excavated with every individual
  sherd recorded separately in three dimensions. The refits between sherds were
  then reconstructed inside a 3D GIS and placed back into the stratigraphy.

  WHAT IT SHOWS: micro-histories of single vessels - and those show that the
  sequence of destruction comprised at least two separate artefact depositions
  rather than one event. The authors write that for Chalcolithic Cyprus the
  results "unequivocally problematize" earlier interpretations of house
  destruction, and call for a reassessment of the wider burnt building
  phenomenon.

  BACKGROUND, NOT FROM THIS PAPER: the site has been excavated since 2015 by
  Leiden University and the University of Cyprus with the Department of
  Antiquities, ahead of tourist development. The excavator's remark that a
  mudbrick building is hard to burn and that fuel must have been brought in
  comes from press coverage of the excavation, not from this article. The
  statement that houses here were burned when already derelict, and that the
  ground was often used again afterwards, comes from the project page of the
  successor project IGNITE (University of Cyprus, 2025-2026). The film attributes
  it to the project, not to the paper.

  WHAT IS NOT CLAIMED: that the house burned twice. Two is the number of
  depositions, not of fires. And no date is given for this particular building;
  the film says "Chalcolithic", and the graphic that shows the period marks the
  range as belonging to the period rather than to the building.


ITEM 2 - THREE CAVES THAT WERE NEVER ONE THING

  Saladie, P., Rodriguez-Hidalgo, A., Marginedas, F., Solari, A. and
  Botella, M. C. (2026).
  Multiphase human cannibalism in Neolithic southern Iberia: Chronological and
  taphonomic evidence from Malalmuerzo, Cariguela, and Majolicas Caves.
  Journal of Archaeological Science 194, 106666. October 2026 issue;
  Crossref record created 25 August 2026.
  doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2026.106666

  WHAT WAS DONE: three caves near Granada have been cited as textbook cases of
  Neolithic cannibalism for decades - without direct radiocarbon dates and
  without a standardised study of how the bones were damaged. This paper
  supplies both: standardised taphonomic and demographic analysis together with
  AMS radiocarbon dating of selected individuals from all three assemblages.

  WHAT IT SHOWS: all three carry signs of systematic perimortem processing
  compatible with cannibalism - cut marks from butchery, deliberate cranial
  breakage, marrow extraction, boiling, human tooth marks. But each site has its
  own signature. Malalmuerzo: a life-like demographic profile, blunt-force
  cranial trauma, dates clustering tightly in the Early Neolithic at roughly
  5300-4950 cal BC, consistent with a short concentrated episode that may have
  involved interpersonal violence. Cariguela: repeated body processing including
  skull-cup manufacture, across different phases of the Neolithic. Majolicas:
  Late Neolithic, roughly 3950-3760 cal BC, less intensive long-bone
  fragmentation, and human remains stained with cinnabar.

  THE AUTHORS' OWN SUMMARY, quoted in the film: neither uniform nor synchronous,
  but expressed through recurrent and context-dependent episodes.

  WHAT THE FILM IS CAREFUL ABOUT: two of the Cariguela dates support recurrence;
  a third is markedly older and, in the authors' words, "remains difficult to
  interpret". An earlier draft of the script turned that into "the authors
  cannot interpret it". It was corrected before recording - between difficult
  and impossible lies exactly the distinction this film is about. The timeline
  graphic gives Cariguela no endpoints for the same reason: none are published.


ITEM 3 - THE DROUGHT THAT DOES NOT EXPLAIN THE PATTERN

  McLellan, A., Metcalfe, S. E., Rushton, E. A. C., Akers, P. D.,
  Barreda Bautista, B. de la, Mardero, S., Ambrogio Gali, O., Schrodt, F. and
  Endfield, G. H. (2026).
  Ancient Maya settlement abandonment and climate instability in Belize
  (700-1600 CE): A spatial analysis of adaptive resilience.
  Journal of Archaeological Science 193, 106661. September 2026 issue;
  Crossref record created 3 August 2026.
  doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2026.106661

  WHAT WAS DONE: 294 sites in Belize, their abandonment mapped against
  high-resolution palaeoclimate data using relative-risk surfaces and cluster
  analysis, across 700 to 1600 CE.

  WHAT IT SHOWS: severe droughts occurred, and their effect was uneven. Western
  and southern regions were abandoned earlier than the drier north-east, despite
  receiving more annual rainfall. Sites near perennial water such as the New
  River Lagoon stayed occupied for centuries while uplands and the upper reaches
  of the major rivers were given up. The authors conclude that drought severity
  was secondary to regional factors and adaptive strategies.

  WHAT IS MARKED AS INTERPRETATION: wetland management appears in the paper as a
  "potential determinant" of resilience. The film says likely, not proven, and
  says it out loud.

  A NOTE ON THE MAP IN THE FILM: the site positions shown are schematic. The
  count is exact because the count is published; the coordinates are not, and
  drawing 294 invented dots on a real coastline would have made a claim the
  paper does not support. Both graphics say "schematic" on their face.

  A NOTE ON WHERE THIS PAPER WAS FOUND: it circulated first as an SSRN preprint.
  Under this channel's rule for the dispatch format, a preprint is not enough.
  Crossref confirms it as a regular peer-reviewed article in the September issue,
  which is why it is here.


ITEM 4 - THE GLASS THAT KEEPS ITS OWN RECORD

  Schibille, N., Tisseyre, P., Cormier, L. and Neuville, D. R. (2026).
  Multi-analytical study of the effects of secondary working and recycling in
  Roman glass.
  Journal of Archaeological Science 194, 106681. October 2026 issue;
  Crossref record created 27 August 2026.
  doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2026.106681

  WHAT WAS DONE: 146 glass finds from a Roman villa in south-western France,
  analysed by laser ablation ICP mass spectrometry for trace elements, with
  optical absorption and Raman spectroscopy for the state of the iron.

  WHAT IT SHOWS: four primary compositional groups plus three further groups
  produced by recycling and mixing. In primary Roman glass, antimony and
  manganese act as decolourants by promoting the oxidation of the iron that is
  naturally present. On remelting their oxidative capacity diminishes and the
  Fe2+/Fe3+ balance shifts back towards Fe2+ - visible in the optical absorption
  spectra and corroborated by Raman. Only the fourth- to fifth-century HIMT
  glass, showing minimal evidence of recycling, can be attributed reliably to
  local secondary production; the earlier material is presumed to come from
  secondary workshops elsewhere.

  WHAT IS NOT CLAIMED, and the film says so in the commentary: a number of
  remeltings. The paper reports groups, not cycles. The villa is not named in
  the publicly available record either, so the film says "a Roman villa in
  south-western France" and leaves it there.

  BACKGROUND, NOT FROM THIS PAPER: that Roman raw glass was made in the eastern
  Mediterranean is long-established and is not a finding of this study.

ON THE PICTURES

  Every photographic-looking shot in this film is generated - there is no
  photograph of any real find in it, and no real, identifiable object is
  depicted. What is shown is an example of the TYPE: a pottery sherd, a
  fragment of blue-green glass, a cave mouth in limestone. The prompts are in
  the production repository.

  The fifteen drawn graphics carry no AI label because they are not generated:
  they are drawn in code, which is also why the labelling in them is correct.

  No third-party images or footage are used anywhere in this film, so no
  third-party rights apply.
