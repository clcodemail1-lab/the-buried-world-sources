THE BURIED WORLD
Dispatch 13 - The Sickle Was Under Her Chin
How this film was made


WHAT THIS PAGE IS FOR

Every film on this channel gets one of these. It lists what we changed before
broadcast and why, including the things that were wrong in our own drafts. The
source list is the other file; this one is about the process.


THREE CORRECTIONS MADE BEFORE BROADCAST

Two of the three had already passed our fact-check. They were caught later,
while the tracking numbers were being re-checked ahead of the picture work.
That is the part worth saying out loud: a check that has been passed is not the
same as a check that was right.


1. DRAWSKO - "five burials, four with sickles" was wrong, and not only in the
   number

   An earlier draft said that at Drawsko five burials had been excavated, four
   of them with sickles across the throat.

   Both relevant papers say FIVE individuals with a sickle. Polcyn and Gajda
   (Antiquity, 2015) give the population those five come from: a cemetery of
   more than 250 burials, in use across the seventeenth and eighteenth
   centuries. Gregoricka and colleagues (PLoS ONE, 2014) record six deviant
   burials in total, five of them with a sickle, laid across the throat OR the
   abdomen - the second placement understood to open the gut rather than take
   the head.

   The number was the smaller problem. The old wording read as though almost
   every grave opened at Drawsko had held a sickle, which overstates how common
   this practice was by orders of magnitude. The film now gives the
   denominator, because "five out of more than two hundred and fifty" is both
   the accurate finding and the more interesting one: rare, but repeated across
   generations, and always with the same kind of object.

   One further thing we did NOT do: the accessible reports do not set out which
   of the five had the sickle at the throat and which at the abdomen. Our
   on-screen graphic therefore gives both placements as a collective statement
   and does not assign them to individuals. Where an age is not published, it
   says "adult" rather than inventing one.


2. THE OBJECT IN HER MOUTH - a detail we could not support, so we cut it

   An earlier draft said the researchers had ruled out coins "on the size of
   the stain". We could not find that in any accessible source, and a claim
   about how a conclusion was reached is exactly the kind of thing that should
   not be repeated on trust.

   It is gone rather than softened. What the film says is what the study
   reports: traces of copper, manganese and gold on the palate, from an object
   placed in the mouth or nose after death, and no identification of what it
   was.


3. AN EXPLANATION WE ADDED, BECAUSE THE FILM WAS ASKING FOR TRUST

   The film says that the strontium in this woman's enamel does not agree that
   she travelled - which is what makes the contradiction with her genetic
   ancestry interesting. But the draft never said why a tooth can know that,
   which meant the whole passage rested on the viewer taking our word for it.

   So the film now explains it: enamel forms in childhood and is never rebuilt
   afterwards; while it forms it takes up strontium from local water and food;
   and the ratio of strontium isotopes in the ground varies from region to
   region. That is textbook archaeometry, not a finding of this study, and it
   is on screen as a diagram rather than as a drawn tooth.


WHAT IS NOT SHOWN, AND WHY

No human remains appear anywhere in this film. Not a bone, not a skull, not a
tooth, not a burial with a body in it, in any shot - not blurred, not in
shadow, not implied.

Three reasons, and they are not all the same kind of reason:

  - The people here are real, named, published individuals. A generated
    "skeleton" would be an invention about a particular human being.

  - The closing section of this film criticises a 2023 facial reconstruction
    for having been built by deforming the scan of a different person's skull.
    A film that raises that objection cannot then show an invented face or
    skeleton of these same women. It would be doing the thing it complains
    about.

  - This channel does not use gore.

The grave positions are shown as drawn excavation plans instead. That is the
form archaeology itself uses, it explains "wedged under the chin" more
precisely than a picture could, and it is accurate because it is drawn. It is
also why this film carries more diagrams than any other on the channel:
fourteen of its fifty-two shots.

One consequence worth admitting: this decision made a planned production step
unnecessary. We had expected the image model's content filter to block skull
and bone motifs, and had a fallback route prepared. With no such motifs in the
film, there was nothing for it to block.


HOW THE SOURCES WERE CHECKED

Every DOI in the source list was checked against the Crossref record for title,
journal, year, publication type and first author - not merely clicked. We do
this by machine since a DOI in our own working notes once turned out to belong
to a different paper entirely.

The check also improved one point in the film. Our draft called the third
objection to the Venice reading a public statement by an anthropologist at
Pisa. It is more than that: it is a formal Commentary published in the same
journal, two years after the original paper. The film's wording is, if
anything, too cautious.


WHERE THE EVIDENCE IS THINNEST

The child at Lugnano in Teverina. The cemetery, its fifth-century date and the
malaria epidemic behind it are published and long established. The particular
burial - a child of about ten with a stone placed in the mouth, the teeth
having left marks in it - was announced by the University of Arizona in
October 2018 and rests on that announcement and the excavation reporting, not
on a dedicated peer-reviewed paper.

We ran it because the case is eight years old, consistently reported, and the
film claims nothing beyond it. Note what the film does not say: it does not
claim malaria was demonstrated for this child. The haemozoin evidence is from
the cemetery. The sentence in the film is "the bones from that cemetery carry
haemozoin", and the wording is deliberate.


ON THE PICTURES AND THE SOUND

Every photograph-like shot is generated and shows an example of a TYPE - an
iron sickle, a triangular padlock, a limestone pebble - never a real,
identifiable find. The prompts are kept in the production repository, along
with a record of which ones failed our own review and what was sent instead.

One of those failures is worth reporting, because it is the kind of thing that
gets through. A shot of the Lugnano stone was specified as two shallow
depressions side by side in a pale pebble. What came back read unmistakably as
a skull: two dark hollows and an opening below them, in white bony-looking
material. Nothing in the wording was wrong - two symmetrical hollows in a
surface simply make a face, whatever you call them. It was recomposed as a row
of marks along one edge, which is also closer to how a row of teeth would leave
them.

The maps and diagrams are drawn in code, which is why they carry no AI label:
they are not generated, and their labelling is correct rather than plausible.

No third-party images, footage or recordings are used, so no third-party rights
apply. The music was composed for this film.
