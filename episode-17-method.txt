THE BURIED WORLD
Episode 17 - The Mary Rose

How this film was made, and what was corrected before recording

Last updated 5 November 2026


WHY THIS FILE EXISTS

Every episode of this channel is checked line by line against the sources
before it is recorded, and the corrections are published. They are the most
useful thing here: they show where the ordinary version of a story is wrong,
and they show what this film got wrong first.

For the Mary Rose the checking ran in two rounds. The first collected the
published figures; the second went back to the printed primary texts (the
Imperial ambassador's letter of 1545, the chronicle of 1548, Hooker's life
of Sir Peter Carew, the State Papers) and to the Mary Rose Trust's own
pages, which in several places disagree with each other. Eleven statements
in the first draft were cut or rewritten. The table at the end lists every
statement of fact in the film with its source and the date it was checked.


1. THE EYEWITNESS DID NOT SAY "GUST"

The only eyewitness account is a Flemish sailor's, written down by the
Imperial ambassador Francois van der Delft in a letter to Charles V dated
23 and 24 July 1545. In the Calendar of State Papers (Spain, vol. 8, no.
101, p. 190) the sailor says the lowest row of gunports on one side had not
been closed; the ship had fired on that side and was turning to fire from
the other "when the wind caught her sails so strongly as to heel her over,
and plunge her open gunports beneath the water". The word "gust", which the
Trust and most retellings use, is not in the text. The film follows the
Calendar's wording. The letter also gives the numbers the film uses for
that day: about five hundred aboard, "five and twenty or thirty" who
escaped. It does not name the ship; the editor's footnote identifies it.


2. THE CHRONICLE SAYS "VERY LOW", AND FOUR HUNDRED MEN

The chronicle usually cited as Hall's (the 1548 continuation, printed under
Hall's name; Hall's own text ends in 1532) says she was "by to much foly,
drouned", "laden wyth much ordinaunce, and the portes left open, which were
very lowe". The first draft had "low"; the printed text has "very lowe"
(1809 edition, p. 863). The same passage puts "foure hundreth men" aboard,
which is why the film gives the range as four hundred to five hundred rather
than a single figure.


3. WHO SAID "A SORT OF KNAVES"

The line is in John Hooker's life of Sir Peter Carew, written after 1575
and printed in 1857 (ed. Maclean, p. 33). It is Sir George Carew, the vice
admiral, who answers "that he had a sort of knaves whom he could not rule";
Sir Gawen Carew is the one who calls across. The first draft had the line
the other way round, and the popular form "the sort of knaves I cannot rule"
is nobody's wording. Hooker's relationship to Carew is given as uncle by
most writers and as cousin by the Trust; the film says "a kinsman". Hooker
also puts seven hundred men aboard, which no other source does, and he was
not there. The film says all of this.


4. "RASHNESS AND GREAT NEGLIGENCE" IS A COUNCILLOR'S VERDICT, NOT A WITNESS'S

Lord Russell's letter to Paget of 23 July 1545 (State Papers of Henry VIII,
vol. 1, 1830, p. 794) says the ship was "throwghe suche rechenes and great
negligence ... caste awaie". "Rechenes" is read as rashness or
recklessness; the Letters and Papers calendar paraphrases it as "rashness".
Russell was writing from the West Country on reports he had received, and
the film says so.


5. THE FIGURE OF SIXTY MILLION VIEWERS IS AN ESTIMATE

The audience for the raising on 11 October 1982 is given everywhere as sixty
million. It is an estimate, never a count; the film says "estimated".


6. THE LONGBOW COUNT DEPENDS ON WHICH TRUST PAGE YOU READ

The Mary Rose Trust's longbow page gives 172 bows found of 250 listed for
the ship, with over 2,000 complete arrows and about 7,000 fragments. The
Trust's page on the Royal Archer gives 137 whole bows. Both are the Trust's
own figures; the film gives both and says where each comes from. Other
figures in circulation (3,500 arrows) were not found in any source and are
not used.


7. THE AGE PROFILE OF THE CREW WAS CUT TO WHAT IS SOURCED

"Eighty-one per cent under thirty" and "some as young as eleven to thirteen"
appear in Wikipedia, citing Gardiner 2005. They do not appear on the Trust's
pages, and the underlying volume was not consulted. The film says "most of
them young" and "boys in their early teens" (the Trust's page on the Young
Mariner gives 12-18; Shankland et al. 2024 sampled men aged 13-40).


8. WHY SHE SANK: THE HULL STUDY AND THE TRUST DISAGREE, AND THE FILM SAYS SO

Peter Marsden, who led the study of the hull, argued that the 1536 refit left
the ship with more and heavier guns and her lowest gunports less than a
metre above the water, and that guns on both sides were found loaded. The
first draft stated both. Neither could be confirmed in a second source; the
Trust's own page on the sinking says there is "no evidence" the ports were
cut too low and that her armament was "within the safe limit". The film
presents the disagreement as a disagreement and attributes each side. What
the Trust does confirm is that the lids of the gunports on the buried side
lie open.


9. THE ISOTOPE RESULT WAS TIGHTENED TO THE PAPER'S OWN WORDS

Scorrer et al. 2021 examined eight men. Five have values consistent with
childhoods in western Britain; for "as many as three" the paper
"tentatively" suggests warmer, more southerly origins. The individual with
cranial morphology suggestive of African ancestry is one of the five raised
in Britain (the Young Mariner), not the Royal Archer, as a first draft of
the film implied. The Royal Archer is one of the three possible southerners.


10. THE 2024 COLLARBONE STUDY DID NOT MEASURE HANDEDNESS

Shankland et al. 2024 (PLOS ONE) used Raman spectroscopy on the clavicles of
twelve men aged 13-40 and found the chemistry changing with age and
differing by side. The paper states that right-sided preference "was
assumed". The film says it did not measure handedness.


11. THINGS THE FILM DOES NOT SAY

That the ship was named after the king's sister (no document; Childs,
Loades and Marsden all note the absence). That the king watched from
Southsea Castle as fact (the film says "by tradition"). A tonnage (sources
give 500, 600, and 700-800). A hard layer of clay and shells over the wreck
(one source only). That the dog was trapped in the cabin door (the Trust's
page says he probably died outside it). Any face reconstruction as the face
of a named individual.


PICTURES

Four shots are reproductions of public-domain works: the Mary Rose page of
the Anthony Roll (c. 1546, Pepys Library, Magdalene College, Cambridge;
reproduction via Wikimedia Commons from a 2000 printed edition) and
Holbein's portrait of Henry VIII (1537). Under German law (Section 68 UrhG,
in force since 7 June 2021) faithful reproductions of public-domain visual
works carry no related rights; the works themselves are out of copyright.
These four shots carry a shot number but no AI label. Every other photograph-
like shot - the hull in its hall, the objects, the sea, the laboratories -
and every painted scene is generated and labelled on screen. No photograph
of the Mary Rose Trust was used; none of its images are free. The sixteen
diagrams are drawn in code. No generated image shows human remains.
