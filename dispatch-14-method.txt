THE BURIED WORLD
Dispatch 14 - Who Made The Terracotta Army? Look At Their Fingerprints
How this film was made, and what the fact check changed


WHY THIS PAPER

Fridays on this channel have so far carried three or four short excavation
reports at a time. This one carries a single find and a single thought, like
the longer Tuesday films: 79 fingerprints left in the clay of 36 soldiers of
the Terracotta Army, measured and published in October 2026. The thought is
one sentence long. The stamps on the soldiers name the workshop. The
fingerprints name the hands - and the hands were not only those of grown men.


WHAT WAS READ, AND WHAT COULD NOT BE

The paper (Li et al. 2026, Journal of Cultural Heritage) is open access, but
its full text could not be retrieved from here: the publisher's site refuses
automated requests. The authors' own abstract was read in full, and it carries
every number in the film, the age threshold of fifteen, and all four of the
authors' cautions.

Five further details come from news reports that quote the full text: where on
the figures the prints sit, the 91 prints first recorded and the 12 set aside,
the six prints on separately made armour ornaments, the Qin bamboo texts on
apprenticeship, and the inscriptions on Qin lacquerware. Four of these rest on
a single report (ScienceAlert); they are marked as such in the table below. If
any of them turns out to be wrong against the full text, it will be corrected
out loud.

The background on how the soldiers were made comes from a second paper that
WAS read in full: Quinn, Zhang, Xia and Li (2017) in Antiquity, a microscopic
study of the clay.


WHAT THE FACT CHECK CHANGED

1. THE START OF WORK HAS NO SINGLE YEAR. Our draft said work on the mausoleum
   began in 247 BC. That is the year in the 2017 Antiquity paper; other
   standard accounts give 246 BC. The film now says "in the third century BC,
   when the future emperor came to the throne of Qin as a boy of thirteen",
   which both versions support.

2. "SEVEN THOUSAND FACES" WAS WRONG. The figure of around seven thousand in the
   2017 paper counts warriors, generals AND horses. The closing line was about
   faces made to look like somebody, so it now says "thousands of faces".

3. "A BOY OF FOURTEEN" WAS OURS, NOT THE PAPER'S. The overlap between an
   adolescent boy's prints and an adult woman's is in the reports; the age of
   fourteen was an illustration we had added. The film now says "a teenage
   boy".

4. NO SINGLE PRINT IS ASSIGNED. The method estimates the mixture of the whole
   set and cannot say who left any one print. The opening print is therefore
   introduced with "nobody knows whose it is", and no sentence in the film
   attributes any individual print to a child or a woman.

5. THE HEADLINE IS CORRECTED, NOT REPEATED. Several reports said the army may
   have been built "mainly" or "mostly" by women and children. The paper's
   estimate puts adult men at 45 percent of the prints, the largest single
   group, and the authors themselves say their reference data may overestimate
   women and juveniles. The film says this in so many words.

WHAT THE FILM SHOWS

There is no real object in this film. Every photograph-like shot is generated,
labelled on screen, and shows a soldier, a surface or a print of the TYPE
described, not a published object. No generated image shows a legible
inscription; where a stamp is the subject, it is shown worn beyond reading.
Diagrams are drawn in code from the published figures.

Because much of this audience watches on a television, the opening seconds
are built to work without sound - the find first, then a caption naming the
place and the period - and the on-screen text throughout is set larger than in
earlier dispatches.
