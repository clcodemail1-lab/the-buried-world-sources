WHY THIS LIST EXISTS
====================

Every image in the film carries a number in the bottom right corner. This list
resolves each one. Every claim that could be checked was checked before the
film was recorded, and the studies those checks rest on are named at the end.

A news round-up is the format where it is easiest to get carried away, because
each item arrives pre-packaged with a headline. Where this film disagrees with
the headline, it says so on screen. Where a study states a limit, the film
states it too.

If something here is wrong, tell me and I will correct it and say so.

WHERE THE FILM DISAGREES WITH THE HEADLINES
===========================================

1. "Silk did not come from China." Several write-ups of the Uzbekistan study
   imply this. The study says the opposite. The cocoons are Bombyx mori, the
   domesticated Chinese silk moth, and they were found with mulberry charcoal.
   That is the Chinese tradition, found three thousand kilometres west of it
   and two thousand years before the Silk Road. The film says so explicitly.

2. "The oldest silk in the world." What the study claims is narrower and more
   interesting: the oldest preserved COCOONS with DIRECT radiocarbon dates
   anywhere, including China - and the lead author explains why, which is that
   damp ground destroys cocoons and dry ground keeps them. Absence of older
   cocoons in China is a preservation fact, not a historical one.

3. "Ancient people stocked a lake." True, and it is the authors' own
   conclusion - but it is a conclusion, not an observation. Nobody excavated a
   fish being carried. The film marks it as the inference it is, and gives the
   reasoning: the waterfalls, and a lake that could not have had fish.

4. Smallpox death tolls. The Chile study measures two individuals. It carries
   no population figures, so neither does this film.


THE NUMBERS
===========

  3 cocoons, Sapalli Tepe, Uzbekistan, c. 2000 BCE, Bombyx mori, silk protein
    radiocarbon dated directly
  4 wooden fish traps, Lake Tesse, Norway, 854 m; phases c. 5000, c. 3850 and
    c. 2700 BCE - more than 2,300 years of use; ice retreat c. 8400 BCE
  2 individuals, northern Chile, buried c. 1492-1631; variola lineage now
    extinct, diverging from medieval European strains c. 1296

SOURCES
=======

Liu, X. et al. (2026): "Silkworm cultivation predating the Silk Road in
  southern Central Asia (2000 BCE)", Science Advances.
  DOI 10.1126/sciadv.aec8738. - Three cocoons from Sapalli Tepe and two other
  Bronze Age sites on the Surkhan-Darya; silk proteins identified as Bombyx
  mori and directly radiocarbon dated; mulberry charcoal from the same site.
  Washington University in St. Louis, with Uzbek partners.

Lake Tesse fish traps (2026), Antiquity. - Four stationary wooden traps from
  the exposed lakebed at 854 m in Jotunheimen; radiocarbon dating,
  dendrochronology and CT imaging give three phases across more than 2,300
  years; the lake was unreachable for fish, so the trout were introduced.

Nakagome, S. et al. (2026): "The genomic identity of early smallpox in South
  America", Science. DOI 10.1126/science.aee6957. - The first ancient variola
  genomes from the Americas, from two Inca-Colonial individuals in northern
  Chile; an extinct lineage diverging c. 1296 from medieval European strains;
  direct molecular evidence for introduction through European colonisation.


WHAT THIS FILM DOES NOT CLAIM
=============================

It does not claim that any of these three things was invented where it was
found. It does not claim to know why silk production did not persist in
Central Asia - the authors say that is unresolved, and so does the film. It
does not claim that the fish traps are the oldest anywhere, only that the
lake had no fish of its own.

The closing observation - two deliberate transfers that did not last, one
accidental one that did - is an observation about these three cases. It is
not a rule about history.

"We don't know" is a finding, not a failure.
