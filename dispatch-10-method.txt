THE BURIED WORLD
Dispatch 10 - The Animals Were Keeping The Records
How this film was made, and what the fact check changed


WHY THESE FOUR PAPERS

They were not chosen because they are about animals. They were chosen because
each one answers a question about PEOPLE that no person wrote down, and in each
case the evidence is an animal that lived alongside them. That is the whole
frame of the film, and it can be said in one sentence without a technical term
in it: the animals kept the books.

That constraint is deliberate and it is new. The previous dispatch in this
series was built around a frame that needed a specialist word to work at all,
and the channel's own verdict on it was that the subject was uninteresting
because it was too specific. So this one carries a rule: if the idea cannot be
stated in one sentence that a stranger understands, it is not the idea.

The papers came from a search of the current volumes of the Journal of
Archaeological Science, its Reports series, Antiquity, Archaeological and
Anthropological Sciences, the Journal of Anthropological Archaeology and the
International Journal of Osteoarchaeology, filtered to items deposited in the
three weeks before the run. Peer-reviewed publication is a condition: an
excavation announcement without a paper is not material for this series, no
matter how photogenic.


SIX THINGS THE FACT CHECK CHANGED

1. "Twenty-one dogs" became "twenty-one dog samples". The abstract says
   samples. Samples and individuals are not the same count, and the difference
   is exactly the kind of small slide this series is supposed to catch.

2. A sentence saying "wild animals were buried too" was cut. It is a reasonable
   inference from the fourth paper, and it is not what the abstract states. The
   commentary now says only what a grave does and does not establish.

3. The number three went back IN. An earlier draft described animal burials as
   sorting into "distinct kinds of treatment" without a count, which was vaguer
   than the source: the abstract says three general categories. Being vaguer
   than your source is also a form of inaccuracy.

4. No publication month appears anywhere in the film. In the previous dispatch,
   a Crossref field called `published` was read as an event when it was a
   label - Elsevier assigns issue dates months in advance - and the commentary
   had to be re-cut. All four records here again carry only `published-print`,
   so the film names no month at all. A date that is not stated cannot be
   wrong, and the source list explains the situation in full.

5. The lineage labels came out. The dog paper works with named haplogroups, and
   naming them in a nine-minute film would have added a term that the viewer
   has to look up in order to hear the sentence. The commentary says "maternal
   lines" and "the western half of Eurasia".

6. "Nine thousand years ago" was checked against the excavation literature
   rather than against the paper. The paper dates the abandonment horizon to
   the early seventh millennium BC; the Final Pre-Pottery Neolithic occupation
   at Motza is reported at about 7100-6700 cal BC. Both fit the phrase, and the
   source list carries the range.


WHAT THE FILM COULD NOT GET AT

The full texts were not readable from here: ScienceDirect refuses automated
requests. Every figure in the commentary therefore comes from the authors' own
abstracts, with background marked as background. Where a number would have
required the full text, the film says so instead of estimating - how many
animals were in each assemblage, what the three burial categories are called,
which household any cat belonged to.

This is the same limitation as the previous dispatch, and it is stated at the
top of the source list rather than in a footnote.


TWO PICTURES THAT WERE THROWN AWAY

The first version of shot [04] was meant to be a vertical section through an
occupation layer. What came back read as a burial: bones spread across a
horizontal surface, exactly the image this film has no business showing. It was
not cropped or relabelled, it was regenerated with a tighter frame and a
different subject - plaster fragments and charcoal instead of bone.

The first version of shot [33] put its object at the edge of the frame with the
middle empty. Also regenerated rather than salvaged in the edit.

Both follow the same rule the channel has learned the hard way: when an image
keeps coming back wrong, the fix is a different frame or a different object,
never another sentence of prohibition in the prompt.


A TOOL THAT LIED, AND WHAT IT COST

The script that downloads generated images reported both replacements as
present, with file sizes, and they were the OLD images. The call had been made
with image numbers but no addresses, and the loop that reads those arguments
only handled them in pairs, so nothing was forced and nothing was re-fetched.

Two pictures were briefly judged that the model had never produced. The tool
now forces a re-fetch when a number is named on its own, and the incident is
recorded here because it is the second time in this project that a success
message described a FILE rather than an ACTION.


WHAT IS DRAWN AND WHAT IS GENERATED

Seventeen of the forty-seven shots are diagrams drawn in code. They carry no AI
label because nothing about them is generated, and that is also why their
labelling is correct - a picture model cannot be trusted with a number on an
axis.

The diagrams state their own limits on the picture: the map of the southern
Levant says that the count is from the paper and the positions are schematic;
the scatter plots say that they show the shape of a result and carry no
published values; the burial diagram says that the three categories are not
named here.

Every photographic-looking shot is generated, and none of them depicts a real,
identifiable find. No third-party images or footage are used, so no third-party
rights apply.

Corrections are welcome and will be made out loud.
