HOW THIS EPISODE WAS CHECKED

Every figure spoken in this film was traced to a publication and the date
fields of each DOI were read individually, because a journal's print date is
an issue label and not the day a paper appeared. Three statements were cut
during that check rather than softened, and one argument changed direction
entirely. Both are described below.


THE PUBLICATIONS THIS EPISODE RESTS ON

Burger, P., Stacey, R. J., Bowden, S. A., Hacke, M. and Parnell, J. (2016).
  Identification, Geochemical Characterisation and Significance of Bitumen
  among the Grave Goods of the 7th Century Mound 1 Ship-Burial at Sutton Hoo
  (Suffolk, UK). PLOS ONE 11 (12), e0166276.
  Published online 1 December 2016. Open access.
  https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0166276
  -- Read in full. Source of the finding that fragments catalogued for decades
  as Stockholm tar are in fact natural bitumen; that its geochemistry points to
  the Middle East, most probably the Dead Sea region or Syria, with molecular
  analogies to oil-stained rocks near Umm El Tlel and the Jebel Bichri in
  Syria; that the two analysed fragments came from the head and the foot of
  the coffin and are each smaller than a sugar cube; and of the authors'
  conclusion that material acquired over that distance is unlikely to have
  been viewed in utilitarian terms - that it was valued as a prestige raw
  material in its own right, or formed part of a fabricated object that has
  otherwise decayed. Also the source of the statement that no human body was
  found, and that chemical analyses evidenced phosphate enrichment of the
  grave goods.

Fenwick, V. (2023). Sutton Hoo: Re-Imaging the Ship and Chamber.
  The Antiquaries Journal 103, 36-62.
  Published online 22 June 2023; October 2023 issue.
  https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003581523000021
  -- Abstract and published summary. Source of the digital three-dimensional
  recording of the ship's iron fastenings and the extrapolation of the lines of
  the missing rivets; of the reconstruction of the chamber roof from the
  position and damage of the fallen objects; of the proposal that the grave
  goods were passed through a temporary opening in the east wall; of the
  statement that residues of phosphate, Middle Eastern bitumen, tar and tape
  cumulatively suggest embalming practices; and of the conclusion that the
  most valuable object relinquished is deduced to be the ship. Also the source
  of the wording that the man in the chamber is not identifiable and that
  Raedwald is often presumed.

Phillips, C. W. (1940). The Excavation of the Sutton Hoo Ship-burial.
  The Antiquaries Journal 20 (2), 149-202. April 1940 issue.
  https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003581500009677
  -- The excavation report of the man who took over the burial chamber from
  Basil Brown in June 1939. Cited as the primary record of the excavation
  itself; the figures it contains are not quoted here, because the paper was
  not accessible in full.

The Sutton Hoo Ship-Burial (1940). Nature 145, 231-232.
  Published 10 February 1940.
  https://doi.org/10.1038/145231a0
  -- The first scientific notice of the find, under nine months after the
  excavation.

Barker, H. (1950). Unusual Phosphatic Material in the Sutton Hoo Ship Burial.
  Nature 166, 348. Published 26 August 1950.
  https://doi.org/10.1038/166348a0
  -- Cited for its EXISTENCE only. The film states that phosphatic material
  was reported in 1950 and does NOT state what the note concluded, because the
  text sits behind a paywall and was not read.

British Museum, collection pages on the Anglo-Saxon ship burial at Sutton Hoo,
  including the recorded statements of Sue Brunning, Curator of Early Medieval
  European Collections.
  -- Source of the ship's length of 27 m; of the description of the burial as
  the richest intact early medieval grave in Europe; of the wear analysis of
  the sword, from which Brunning concludes the owner wore it on the right and
  drew it with the left hand, and that the mourners laid it on the dead man's
  right; of the garnets' origin in Sri Lanka; of the silver platter stamped in
  Constantinople and already a century old when buried; of the nested set of
  ten silver bowls; of the two silver spoons with Greek lettering, one
  inscription less careful than the other; of the gold belt buckle of over
  400 g; of the helmet lying wrapped in cloth near the left side of the head,
  with only one garnet eyebrow backed with gold foil; and of the curatorial
  wording that the king cannot be named for certain.

Dating of the burial to between 610 and 635 follows the analysis of the
  Merovingian coins by Gareth Williams, Curator of Early Medieval Coinage at
  the British Museum.

The Bromeswell bucket: the 2024 and 2025 excavation campaigns at Sutton Hoo
  were run by the National Trust with FAS Heritage and Time Team. The 2025
  analysis is reported through those organisations and in contemporaneous
  coverage. Source of the complete base of the vessel and the previously
  missing parts of its frieze; of the cremated human and animal bone inside,
  including a skull fragment and part of an ankle; of the unburnt antler comb;
  and of the Greek inscription, "use this in good health, Master Count, for
  many happy years". The attribution of its manufacture to Antioch is given in
  that coverage as probable, and the film says so.

The two reconstructions of the helmet - Herbert Maryon, 1945 to 1947, and
  Nigel Williams, 1970 to 1971, the latter comprising four months to
  disassemble, five months of study and nine months to rebuild - are given on
  secondary authority and are not drawn from a primary publication.


WHERE THIS EPISODE DIFFERS FROM THE USUAL ACCOUNT

- The usual account presents the embalming reading as the current state of
  knowledge. It is one of two published readings of the same material. The
  2016 study of the bitumen itself - the only one of the four residues with a
  dedicated geochemical publication - reaches a different conclusion and
  rejects a utilitarian explanation outright. The film gives both and says
  that neither is settled. This is not a weakness in the evidence; it is what
  the episode is about.
- The usual account dates the chemical answer to the soil analyses of 1967. A
  note on phosphatic material from this grave had already appeared in Nature
  in 1950. The film says that, and no more than that.


WHAT THE FILM DOES NOT CLAIM

- It does not claim that Raedwald was buried there. The two most recent
  authorities both decline to name him: "not identifiable" and "we can't name
  that king for certain". Raedwald is, in the same sentence, "often presumed".
- It does not claim that a body was definitely present. The phosphate is real;
  so is the absence of shroud ties and the arrangement of the objects. The
  cenotaph reading has never gone away, and the film says explicitly that it
  is not going to settle this.
- It does not state what the 1950 Nature note concluded, only that it exists.
- It does not use a rivet count or a total object count. The sources say
  "nearly all" of the rivets survived and give no figure, and the frequently
  quoted total of 263 objects is not borne out by the source checked.
- It does not give the soil pH as a number. The acidity is documented; the
  value is not, in anything read for this film.
- It does not give the ship's beam, depth, plank count or frame count, because
  the primary record for those figures was not accessible.
- It does not date the 2023 study to October. That is the issue label; the
  paper appeared online on 22 June 2023.
- It says the comb MAY carry surviving DNA. That is a stated hope of the
  excavators, not a result.

WHAT THE IMAGES ARE NOT

None of these images is a photograph of a real find. Every one of them was
generated or drawn for this film, and they stand for the things the film
describes rather than depicting particular ones. Where a specific site is
named in the commentary - Bratskii Kamen, the Angara, Smithfield - the image
beside it is a stand-in, not that place.

THIRTY-EIGHT of the 86 shots are drawn rather than generated, and that is the
highest share this channel has ever run. The reason is the subject. Detection
rates set against each other, pedigrees with carriers marked, two age
distributions, a phylogeny with a polytomy, three maps and two timelines are
statements an image model cannot make without inventing them. They are drawn
in Python, and every one of them carries its source in its own footer.

The drawn distributions are SCHEMATIC and say so on the image itself. The
curves illustrate what the paper reports - a mortality peak at 7.5 to 11 years
and the scarcity of deaths between 20 and 35 - and they carry no counts,
because the paper publishes none in that form.

The maps show ENDPOINTS and distances, not routes. The film does not claim a
migration path: the authors argue explicitly for a continent-wide rodent
reservoir rather than a continuous chain of human contact, and drawing a line
of travel would have contradicted the very finding the act rests on.


A NOTE ON WHAT IS NOT SHOWN

The victims of these outbreaks were mostly children between seven and eleven
years old. There is no depiction of a sick, dying or dead person anywhere in
this film - not photographic, not painted, not implied. Where the commentary
speaks about the children, the picture is a grave plan or an age
distribution, because a drawn grave number is precisely what survives of
them, and a painted face would be an invention at the one place where the
film can least afford one.

The shot that accompanies the line "there is nothing to illustrate here" is an
empty gravel terrace above a grey lake, and it carries no sound bed either.
