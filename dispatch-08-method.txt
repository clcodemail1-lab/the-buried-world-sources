THE BURIED WORLD
Dispatch 08 - The Carrier Was Never The Message
How this film was made


WHY THESE FOUR

The week produced more than four usable papers. These four were chosen because
they share a shape: in every one of them the object had already been described -
sometimes for a century - and the new information came out of something the
object was carrying. A mineral layer under paint. A film on a blade. Teeth in a
jar. DNA in soil where no object was left at all.

Two cases were dropped for reasons worth stating. A study of incense residues at
Pompeii fits the same shape and is good work, but it was published in spring and
collides with a Pompeii film we have scheduled for October. A study of Sanxingdui
sediments was dropped because we have already spent Sanxingdui on a flagship
episode, and repeating a site inside the same year makes the channel look larger
than it is.


THE FACT CHECK, AND WHAT IT CHANGED

Every number, name and claim was checked against the papers before the script was
recorded. Six things were cut or rewritten:

1. "Made with childbirth blood." Several outlets ran this as the finding. The
   measurement is human serum protein in the binder. The childbirth attribution
   is the authors' own proposal, phrased conditionally. The script states the
   measurement and marks the proposal as a proposal. The thumbnail does not
   carry it at all.

2. "Thirty-seven people in a jar." Stated flat, this claims a precision the
   paper does not have. The minimum number of individuals is fourteen counted
   from left upper arm bones, nineteen from right thigh bones and skulls, and
   thirty-seven from teeth. All three are in the film, and the drawn graphic
   carries all three.

3. "Ninth to twelfth century." Rounded centuries are tidier than the evidence.
   The paper gives c. cal AD 890-1160 from eight directly dated human bones.
   The film gives the span and the number of samples. The graphic draws ONE
   span, not eight invented ones - the individual ranges are not available to
   us, and drawing eight bars would have been a clean-looking chart made of
   guesses.

4. "Proof of surgery under anaesthesia." The authors set their own limits: the
   aconitine may have partially hydrolysed over six centuries, and sampling was
   restricted. What exists is a compound identified on an instrument. The script
   says exactly that, and says what it does not say.

5. "A giant predator." The fossil is by far the largest known plagiosaurid and
   the first read as a top predator - both statements are inside that one
   family. It is not a giant among Triassic animals. The script draws the
   distinction out loud, and the graphic compares it only with its own family.

6. "2.7 metres." The paper's abstract says "exceeding 2 metres". The 2.7 comes
   from an estimate combining the skull with the 1948 photographs. Both figures
   are in the film, labelled for what they are.

One more check changed nothing on screen but is worth recording. The Mongolian
paper was first visible to us only as a preprint. Our rule is that a preprint is
not dispatch material. The Crossref record shows it as a peer-reviewed article in
the September issue, so it ran.

And one embarrassment, since this channel publishes its corrections: a DOI in our
own working notes for the Ming paper pointed at a completely different article -
an existing, resolvable DOI belonging to someone else's work on architectural
practice. It looked right because it opened something. It was caught by checking
every DOI against the Crossref record by machine rather than by clicking it. That
is now the standard for every source list we publish.


WHAT IS NOT SHOWN

No human remains appear in this film. The third case is a jar containing the
remains of at least thirty-seven people, including small children. There is no
bone, no skull, no tooth, no open jar and no burial anywhere on screen, not even
suggested or out of focus. The site is shown from the surface, and the count is a
drawn graphic.

No blood is shown either. The first case is about blood in a mortar; what appears
on screen is what was measured - a thin section under a microscope and a grey
mineral layer.

No operation, no wound and no patient appears in the second case. The instrument,
the plant and the measuring bench do the work.

The 1948 photographs from Bear Island are not shown. They are third-party
material under copyright. What appears is a generated reconstruction of an
expedition scene and a generic stack of old prints - not a copy of any particular
photograph.


THE PICTURES

Every photographic image in this film is generated, and every one is labelled
"AI RECONSTRUCTION" on screen for its full duration. The official EU marking
appears in the first fifteen seconds. Nine images are not generated at all: the
diagrams are drawn in code, because image models place lettering unreliably and a
chart with invented labels is worse than no chart.

No real, identifiable object is depicted. Where the script names a specific find -
the scissors from Xia Quan's tomb, Jar 1 at Site 75, the skeleton from Bjornoya -
what you see is an object of that type, not that object.

Five images were rejected during quality control and remade. Three of them were
remade with a different SUBJECT rather than a different wording, because the
model kept returning the same wrong thing: a laboratory cross-section that came
back twice as a landscape soil profile, sample vials that came back twice looking
like blood samples, and a sandstone jar that came back twice as a smooth white
ceramic vase. A sixth image was remade because it showed a finned, long-snouted
animal, which would have contradicted the narration in the same breath.


THE SOUND

The music was composed for this episode and exists nowhere else. Ambient beds and
effects come from licensed libraries. One sound carries an action: lime curing,
under the graphic that explains the binder. Everything else is places.

Two sounds were deliberately not used. There is ice in our library and the
excursus is set in the Arctic - but Bear Island is ice-free in summer and the
expedition worked on wet rock, so ice would have invented a season. There is a
raptor call that fits a steppe horizon - but Yumin is a lakeside settlement, and
in the Arctic a raptor would simply be the wrong bird.


CORRECTIONS

If something here is wrong, we would like to know. Corrections are pinned under
the video and carried into this file.
