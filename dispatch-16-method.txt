THE BURIED WORLD
Dispatch 16 - This Ethiopian Palace Could Have Stood 16 Floors High
How this film was made, and what the fact check changed


WHY THIS PAPER

Fridays on this channel now carry a single find and a single thought. The
find here is a wall at Yeha, in northern Ethiopia, with square holes in it
where wooden beams burned away more than two thousand years ago - the
ground floor of the palace Grat Be'al Gibri, built around 800 BC. The thought
is one sentence long: one floor is left, and the walls could have carried
sixteen. That is the result of the first structural calculation ever run on
this way of building, published in July 2026, and it is why the engineers
think the building did not fall under its own weight.


WHAT WAS READ

The paper (Drieschner and Schnelle 2026, Heritage) is open access under
CC BY 4.0 and was read in full, including the sections on geometry, loads,
the deterministic and the uncertainty results, and the discussion with the
authors' own list of what is still open. Every figure in the film is in it.
The press release of the Brandenburg University of Technology (25 September
2026) and two news reports (scinexx, 29 September; HeritageDaily, 1 October)
were read for the way the number travelled - the film says where they
differ from the paper. Background on the kingdom of the period comes from
reference works and is marked as such.


WHAT THE FACT CHECK CHANGED

1. "THE WIDTH OF A HAND" BECAME "A HAND'S SPAN". The beam impressions measure
   about 21 to 28 centimetres, most of them 24. A hand is about ten
   centimetres wide; a hand's span is about twenty.

2. "TWO GERMANS" BECAME "TWO RESEARCHERS IN GERMANY". The paper gives
   institutions, not nationalities.

3. THE DOORWAY WALL GETS NO NUMBER OF ITS OWN. The paper derives sixteen
   floors from the worst case of the wall corner (scaling factor 19, divided
   by 1.2). The doorway's worst case is 23, which would give about nineteen -
   but the authors do not state that figure, so the film says "a little
   more".

4. NINETEEN VERSUS SIXTEEN. The university's press release said "at least 19
   storeys"; the paper's discussion says 16. A scaling factor of 19 for the
   wall corner stands in the paper; 19 divided by the authors' load factor
   of 1.2 is 15.8. The film reports both numbers and the arithmetic that
   links them, and attributes the link to the figures in the paper rather
   than to a statement by the authors, who do not comment on the release.

5. NOTHING IN THE FILM CLAIMS A NUMBER OF FLOORS. The film says sixteen is a
   ceiling on what the walls could bear, not a count of what stood on them,
   and that the excavators' reconstruction still shows eight. That is the
   authors' own position.

WHAT THE FILM SHOWS

No photograph of the Yeha excavation is used. Every photograph-like shot is
generated and labelled on screen; it shows a wall, a pillar, a landscape of
the TYPE described, not a published image. The reconstructions of the
palace are painted, labelled, and follow the written description in the
paper (sixty metres square, six-metre podium, six pillars, eight floors in
the excavators' drawing) without copying its figures. Diagrams are drawn in
code from the paper's figures and tables; every stress field in them is
schematic.
