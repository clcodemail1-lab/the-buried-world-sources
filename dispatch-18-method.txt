THE BURIED WORLD
Dispatch 18 - 125,000 Years Ago, Someone Knew the Poison Tree
How this film was made, and what the fact check changed


WHY THIS PAPER

Fridays on this channel carry a single find and a single thought. The find
here is a stone point from the shore of a lake that dried up long ago, in
the Mundafan basin of southern Saudi Arabia, about 125,000 years old. On its
tip, chemists found compounds that point to the sap of Acokanthera
schimperi, known in Africa as the arrow poison tree. The thought: whoever
stood on that shore knew the poison tree, and what they did with it the
stone tells only by half. In January 2026 a study from South Africa had put
the oldest known poison on a hunting weapon at 60,000 years. The Arabian
paper was quickly reported as doubling that record. The film tests the
headline against the paper's own results.


WHAT WAS READ

The paper (Clarkson et al. 2026, Science 394, 746-751) was read in full on
26 November 2026, together with the supplementary materials (156 pages),
which were read for the luminescence dating, the lithic and residue sections
and the summary of the chemical results. The paper is not open access; the
full text was obtained through a university library. The authors' data set
on Zenodo (doi:10.5281/zenodo.21927501, CC BY 4.0) was downloaded on 26
November 2026; its residue photographs appear in the film. For the
comparison, the abstract of Isaksson, Hoegberg and Lombard (2026, Science
Advances) was read through Crossref; the full text was not needed for the
two facts the film takes from it (age and source plant). News reports were
read only to see how the result travelled.


WHAT THE FACT CHECK CHANGED

1. SEVEN TOOLS, BUT ONE POINT. The abstract says that several points carry
   poison residues. The supplement is more precise: seven artifacts carry
   compounds most likely from the latex of Acokanthera schimperi, and for
   six of them the distribution of the residue suggests working the wood of
   the tree or harvesting its latex. One point carries the latex on its tip
   together with compounds of animal origin. The film is built on that
   difference. An early draft said the six were "most likely" used on the
   tree; the supplement says they "may have been", and the film now says
   they "seem to have been".

2. NO ARROWS. The paper speaks of hafted weapon tips in composite hunting
   systems. It does not mention arrows or bows, and it says that the
   fractures cannot tell thrusting from throwing. The film says "points
   bound to shafts" and "nothing here shows a bow".

3. "THE OLDEST" IS THE SUPPLEMENT'S CLAIM, NOT THE PAPER'S. The phrase "the
   oldest evidence of hunting poisons in the world" appears in the
   supplementary text, not in the paper. The film quotes it as the
   supplement's line and sets the paper's own limits against it. The
   comparison record (Umhlatuzana, 60,000 years, Science Advances, January
   2026) was checked against Crossref.

4. NOT "HOMO SAPIENS". No hominin fossils have been found at the site. The
   film says "people" and leaves open who they were; it mentions the
   footprints in the Nefud desert as the paper does, as a record of people
   in the same warm period, not as proof of who stood on this shore.

5. NOT PROOF OF HUNTING. The paper says that neither the spatial association
   of bones and tools nor the age structure of the animals demonstrates
   hunting. The film says so twice.

6. "MOST LIKELY", NOT "CERTAINLY", ACOKANTHERA. The supplement writes that
   the compounds (among them strophanthidol and ouabagenin) are most likely
   from the latex of Acokanthera schimperi. The film says the compounds
   "point to" the plant.

7. THE RESIN HAS THREE CANDIDATES. The paper names Balanites aegyptiaca
   (desert date), Cassia angustifolia (senna) or native Pistacia. An early
   draft named two; the film names all three.

8. THE DRY PHASES. An early draft said there was "desert between and after"
   the two lake phases. The paper says the lake was one hydrological pulse
   within a longer arid sequence; the film uses that wording.

WHAT THE FILM SHOWS

The residue micrographs and residue maps are photographs from the authors'
data set on Zenodo (Chris Clarkson, CC BY 4.0). They carry no AI label.
Every other photograph-like shot is generated and labelled on screen; it
shows a landscape, an animal or an object of the TYPE described, not a
published find. The figures of the paper itself are not reproduced. Maps
and charts are drawn in code from the paper's text.
