WHY THIS LIST EXISTS
====================

Every image in the film carries a number in the bottom right corner. This list
resolves each one. Every figure, name and date was checked against specialist
sources before the film was recorded, and the works those checks rest on are
named at the end.

This site attracts more confident nonsense than almost any other in
archaeology. That obliges the film to be unusually exact about its own footing.
Where a figure is contested, this list says so; where a figure was cut from the
script because the sources would not carry it, it says that too.

If something here is wrong, tell me and I will correct it and say so.


THE IMAGES
==========

All 63 shots were made for this film. No photograph of the site, of any
excavation, of any laboratory or of any museum object appears anywhere in it,
and no third-party images are used. Photographs of public-domain objects are
still protected in Germany (Section 72 UrhG); rather than borrow what cannot be
licensed, this channel draws what the published record describes.

Two kinds of images appear, and the film keeps them apart on purpose:

  RECONSTRUCTION - AI-generated, marked "AI RECONSTRUCTION" on screen for as
                   long as it is visible, alongside the EU marking for
                   AI-generated content. Photographic-looking shots show place,
                   find and laboratory; painted shots show people and actions,
                   which are always interpretation.

  DIAGRAM        - the timeline, the two maps, the city plan, the pit plan, the
                   reassembled object, the four-part explanation table and the
                   Sanxingdui/Jinsha comparison. Drawn in code from published
                   coordinates and figures, not generated. They carry no AI
                   marking because none is generated.

A note on the masks: no bronze in this film is a copy of a specific museum
object. The shapes follow the published descriptions - the rectangular eyes
with cylindrical pupils, the swept-back ears, the mouth drawn wide - because
that is what the record supports. They are reconstructions, and they are marked
as such.

THE DIAGRAMS
============

         founder king his protruding eyes was written around AD 355. The gap is
         drawn as a gap.
  1:30  Real coordinates, no coastlines. Sanxingdui to Anyang is about
         1,100 km in a straight line - the script said "two thousand" in draft
         and was corrected when this map was drawn.
  4:03  The walled area, the south wall, the river standing in for a north
         wall, and the 2024 jade workshop. Schematic; no streets are invented.
  6:26  Deliberately not a map. The origins are documented, the routes are
         not.
  7:04  Eight pits, drawn to relative size. No pit carries an area figure
         because the published figure is a range (3.5-20 m2).
  8:17  The reassembled object: a vessel rim from Pit 2, a kneeling figure
         from Pit 3, a beast from Pit 8.
  9:49  10:31  11:14  11:41  The four explanations, each with
         what it accounts for and what it leaves standing.
  12:45  What Jinsha continues and what it changes.


WHAT WAS CUT
============

  1. The number of elephant tusks. Sources say "over 100", "over 700" and
     "more than 1,000". The film says "hundreds".
  2. Earlier radiocarbon ranges (1100-1050 BC and others). The film uses only
     the 2025 result, with its interval and its probability.
  3. "The bronze tree is over four metres tall." It is 396 cm as restored, and
     the top is missing. The film says "just under four metres".
  4. "There is no writing at Sanxingdui." There are incised signs on the gold
     staff and on pottery; what is missing is a readable system. The film says
     that instead.
  5. "No graves." A cemetery is under excavation at Renshengcun. The film says
     no royal tombs have been identified.
  6. "The civilisation vanished in a flood." Not supported. Act five says the
     opposite, with Jinsha as the evidence.

SOURCES AND FURTHER READING
===========================

Sichuan Provincial Institute of Cultural Relics and Archaeology, with Peking
  University (announced at the Sanxingdui Forum, Deyang, September 2025):
  radiocarbon dating of Pits 3, 4, 6 and 8. - 95.4 per cent probability that
  the pits were filled between 1201 and 1012 BC, placing the deposition in the
  late Shang period. This is the only dating figure the film uses.

Li Haichao; Ran Honglin; Xu Feihong et al. (2023): "New discoveries at the
  Sanxingdui Bronze Age site in south-west China", Antiquity 97(391). - The
  2020-22 excavation of Pits 3 to 8: rectangular pits between about 3.5 and
  20 square metres, dug inside sealed climate-controlled enclosures; the count
  of over 900 objects from the 1986 pits; the layering of ivory above bronze.

Newly discovered sacrificial pits at the Sanxingdui site: insights into Bronze
  Age ritual remains in Southwest China (2025), Archaeological Research in
  Asia. - The excavation report the film follows for the sequence of filling
  and for the cross-pit joins.

Zhu Bingquan et al. (2016): "Highly radiogenic lead isotopic signature in
  Chinese Shang and Zhou bronzes", Scientific Reports 6:23304. - The unusual
  lead signature shared by Sanxingdui bronzes and bronzes from the Shang
  capital at Anyang. The film says "the same signature", not "the same mine".

Evidence of the use of silk by a Bronze Age civilisation for sacrificial
  purposes in the Yangtze River basin of China (2024), Scientific Reports 14.
  - Silk fibroin identified by immunoaffinity enrichment and ELISA in the ash
  layers and in mineralised fabric traces on bronze and jade. What had been
  read as ash on the gold masks is textile.

Lin, Xiangxiong; Wang, Shuhong et al. (2017), Geoarchaeology 32. - A major
  earthquake in the Chengdu basin between about 1600 and 1200 BC, proposed as
  a trigger for the end of the Sanxingdui centre. The film gives this as one of
  four readings and notes that the published dating for the pits is a
  two-century window, not a date.

Sun Hua (Peking University), quoted in Archaeology, November/December 2024,
  "The Many Faces of the Kingdom of Shu". - The reading of the bronze figures
  as two distinct groups, distinguished by hairstyle, and of the pits as the
  losing side of a struggle between a priestly and a secular elite. Given in
  the film as a reading, not a finding; the circulating percentage split is not
  used.

Jay Xu (Asian Art Museum, San Francisco), same article. - Destroying deities
  held to have failed and making new ones is a documented practice in early
  China. Paraphrased, not quoted.

Chang Qu, Huayang Guo Zhi (Chronicles of Huayang), c. AD 355. - The Shu
  chapter, and the founder king Cancong whose eyes protruded. The film uses
  this as the popular explanation it then sets aside: the text is roughly
  1,400 years younger than the pits and works from legend.

Jade and stone workshop at Sanxingdui, announced July 2024 by the Sichuan
  Provincial Institute. - About one kilometre north of the sacrificial area;
  over 400 features identified since 2022.

Golden Sun Bird, Jinsha site, Chengdu (excavated 2001; adopted as the emblem
  of China's cultural heritage by the State Administration of Cultural
  Heritage on 16 August 2005).
