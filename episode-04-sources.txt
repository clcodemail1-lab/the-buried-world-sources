WHY THIS LIST EXISTS
====================

Every image in the film carries a number in the bottom right corner. This list
resolves each one. Every claim that could be checked was checked before the
film was recorded, and the works those checks rest on are named at the end.

This is an episode that spends its fourth act correcting things people
confidently repeat. That obliges it to be unusually exact about its own
footing. Where a figure is contested, this list says so, and where a figure was
cut from the script, it says that too.

If something here is wrong, tell me and I will correct it and say so.


THE IMAGES

All 63 shots were made for this film. No photographs of Roman structures, of
any excavation, of any laboratory or of any museum object appear anywhere in
it, and no third-party images are used. Photographs of public-domain objects
are still protected in Germany (Section 72 UrhG); rather than borrow what
cannot be licensed, this channel draws what the published record describes.

Two kinds of images appear, and the film keeps them apart on purpose:

  RECONSTRUCTION - AI-generated, marked "AI RECONSTRUCTION" on screen for as
                   long as it is visible, alongside the EU marking for

  DIAGRAM        - the two maps, the two reaction panels, the procedure
                   comparison, the healing cycle, the sea panel, the property

DIAGRAMS - WHAT THEY REST ON
============================

The map at 2:12 uses real coordinates, linearly projected, with the
latitude axis stretched by 1/cos(41.5 deg). No coastlines are drawn and no
distance is given: the map claims only where the places are. The Phlegraean
Fields are not marked separately from Puteoli and Baiae, because on this scale
they are the same ground.

The map at 8:44 marks the ROMACONS drilling sites. Cosa and Santa Liberata
are about twelve kilometres apart and appear as one point with both names -
two points there would be a precision this map does not have.

The property table at 11:39 deliberately carries NO figures in megapascals.
Published compressive strengths for Roman wall concrete scatter across roughly
6, 10-30 and about 20 MPa depending on what was sampled and how, against
roughly 50 MPa for ordinary modern structural concrete. The comparison the film
makes - that Roman concrete is not close, on the property engineers design to -
survives that scatter. A single number would not, so the table says "low" and
"high" and leaves it there.

The healing cycle at 7:09 is a schematic of the mechanism described in
Seymour et al. 2023. It is not to scale and it is not a micrograph.


WHAT THIS FILM CORRECTS
=======================

Three claims are widely repeated and are wrong. Each is taken apart in the film
and each is sourced here.

  "The recipe was lost."
      Vitruvius, De architectura 2.6, names the material and where to get it.
      The text has been in print since the editio princeps (Sulpizio / Silber,
      Rome, 1486/87) and has never been unavailable since.

  "We cannot reproduce it."
      Seymour et al. 2023 cast lime-clast mortar in the laboratory and
      demonstrated self-sealing. DMAT, co-founded by the paper's senior author,
      has since brought a lime-clast cement to market in Italy and Switzerland.
      Whether that product performs as advertised over decades is an open
      commercial question; that the chemistry can be reproduced is not.

  "It is better than modern concrete."
      Not on compressive strength, and not by any margin. What it is better at
      is lasting without maintenance in the absence of steel reinforcement -
      which is a different property, achieved under different constraints.


WHAT WAS CUT AND WHY
====================

Five things were removed or changed at the fact-check stage:

  1. Every compressive strength in megapascals - the published figures scatter
     too widely to name one. The comparison is made in words instead.
  2. "Cement causes 8 % of global CO2 emissions." The 8 % figure is widely
     quoted and does not sit comfortably against the roughly 1.47 billion
     tonnes recorded for cement manufacture in 2024. The film gives no
     percentage.
  3. "Concrete is the second most used substance on Earth after water." A
     popular formulation that is hard to source cleanly. Replaced with the
     defensible version: no manufactured material is used in greater quantity.
  4. A sentence saying that Vitruvius never mentions hot mixing. He may. The
     paper itself discusses his use of "macerata" for plaster lime against
     "extincta" for structural lime and considers the second compatible with
     hot mixing. The film now says the sources are ambiguous exactly where it
     matters, which is both true and more interesting.
  5. The planned thumbnail line, "WE CAN'T COPY THIS" - contradicted by the
     film's own fourth act.

One date was corrected rather than cut: ROMACONS was established in 2001, but
the drilling campaigns ran from 2003 to 2007. The film says 2003.

SOURCES AND FURTHER READING
===========================

Seymour, Linda M.; Maragh, Janille; Sabatini, Paolo; Di Tommaso, Michel;
  Weaver, James C.; Masic, Admir (2023): "Hot mixing: Mechanistic insights into
  the durability of ancient Roman concrete", Science Advances 9(1), eadd1602,
  6 January 2023. DOI 10.1126/sciadv.add1602. - The backbone of acts two and
  five. Samples from bedding mortar in the city wall at Privernum, collected in
  summer 2016. Lime clasts formed at high temperature, consistent with
  quicklime mixed directly with pozzolan and water. Self-healing demonstrated
  on 10 cm cylinders fractured and held 0.5 mm apart under flowing water:
  sealed within one to three weeks, control specimens never sealed. Note the
  authors' own hedging - hot mixing is raised as a proposition, not proved as
  an intention.

Jackson, Marie D.; Mulcahy, Sean R.; Chen, Heng; Li, Yao; Li, Qinfei;
  Cappelletti, Piergiulio; Wenk, Hans-Rudolf (2017): "Phillipsite and
  Al-tobermorite mineral cements produced through low-temperature water-rock
  reactions in Roman marine concrete", American Mineralogist 102(7), 1435-1450.
  - Act three. Seawater percolating through the concrete dissolves volcanic
  glass; alkaline fluids grow interlocking platy minerals in the pores, which
  resist fracture. The increase is in toughness, not in compressive strength.

Jackson, Marie D. et al. (2021, with Masic and colleagues): "Reactive binder
  and aggregate interfacial zones in the mortar of Tomb of Caecilia Metella
  concrete, 1C BCE, Rome", Journal of the American Ceramic Society. - The
  ancient evidence for long-term mineral reworking in a terrestrial monument,
  and the source of the calcite crack-filling observation cited in the film.
    doi:10.1111/jace.18133

Jackson, Marie D. et al. (2009): "Assessment of material characteristics of
  ancient concretes, Grande Aula, Markets of Trajan, Rome", Journal of
  Archaeological Science 36(11). - Aggregate chosen by function; stratlingite
  in the wall mortars; unit weights of about 1750 kg/m3 for wall mortar against
  1430-1640 for the pumice-bearing vault mortars.
    doi:10.1016/j.jas.2009.07.011

Brandon, Christopher J.; Hohlfelder, Robert L.; Jackson, Marie D.;
  Oleson, John P. (2014): Building for Eternity: The History and Technology of
  Roman Concrete Engineering in the Sea, Oxbow Books. - The ROMACONS project:
  cores 0.09 m in diameter and up to 5.8 m long, drilled 2003-2007 at Cosa,
  Santa Liberata, Caesarea, Baiae and Portus Iulius, Alexandria and
  Chersonissos.
    ISBN 9781789256369

Vitruvius, De architectura 2.6.1-2 (c. 25 BC). - The powder found near Baiae
  and around Vesuvius which, mixed with lime and rubble, gives strength to a
  structure and sets under water. Editio princeps: Giovanni Sulpizio da Veroli
  for Eucharius Silber, Rome, 1486/87.

Pliny the Elder, Naturalis Historia 35.166 (c. AD 77). - The dust of the hills
  of Puteoli which, on meeting the waves, becomes a single stone the sea cannot

Sannino, Filomena et al. (2023): "Early exploitation of Neapolitan pozzolan

  Scientific Reports 13. - Evidence for the long-distance trade in the ash.

Smeaton, John (1791): A Narrative of the Building ... of the Eddystone

On reinforcement as the limiting factor in modern concrete life: the standard

WHAT THE FILM DOES NOT CLAIM
============================

The title says the concrete is still healing itself, and the film means that
narrowly: a mechanism identified in ancient mortar and reproduced in the
laboratory continues to operate where water reaches a fractured lime clast. It
does not claim that Roman concrete is indestructible, that it outperforms
modern concrete, that the recipe was lost, or that anything about it is
unexplained.

It does not claim that the Romans knew what they were doing. That is the one
question left open at the end, and it is left open because the evidence leaves
it open - the clasts establish the process, not the intention, and the written
sources are ambiguous precisely there.

"We don't know what they intended" is a finding, not a failure.
