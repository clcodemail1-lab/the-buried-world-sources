ITEM 1 - BLOOD IN THE BINDER, HUASHAN

  Wu, M., Hu, P., Zhai, K., Weng, X., Wang, Y. et al. (2026).
  Blood-induced biomineralized calcium carbonate used for binding in the
  Huashan rock art of Guangxi, Southern China.
  Journal of Archaeological Science 193, 106650.
  doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2026.106650

  WHAT WAS MEASURED: scanning electron microscopy of individual paint layers,
  spectroscopy for mineral and chemical composition, and testing for organic
  binders. The pigment is a red clay rich in nano-scale hematite; the binder is
  a composite mortar of lime and blood. Blood proteins induce biomineralized
  calcium carbonate which encapsulates the hematite and bonds it to the
  limestone. The art spans more than eighty sites over some 260 km and is at
  least 2,000 years old.

  WHAT WAS INTERPRETED: some of the detected proteins led the authors to
  propose that part of the human blood may have been collected from women
  during or after childbirth, and to connect this to fertility rites. The
  wording in the paper is conditional. The film says what was measured, and
  marks the proposal as a proposal. Several news outlets reported it as
  established; it is not, and our thumbnail does not carry it at all.


ITEM 2 - ACONITINE ON MEDICAL INSTRUMENTS, JIANGYIN

  Ling, X., Li, J., Zhao, G., Cao, X., Weng, X., Zhang, H., Li, Z. and
  Zhao, C. (2026). Surgical anaesthesia in Ming China: scientific analysis of
  aconitine residues on medical instruments.
  Antiquity 100(412), 1088-1106.
  doi.org/10.15184/aqy.2026.10347

  WHAT WAS MEASURED: two instruments - scissors and tweezers, finds 28-11 and
  28-4, both 123 mm long - from the tomb of the physician Xia Quan (1348-1411),
  excavated in 1974 at Jiangyin. X-ray fluorescence, micro-Raman spectroscopy
  and stimulated Raman scattering microscopy identify aconitine, the alkaloid
  of Aconitum.

  THE AUTHORS' OWN LIMITS, which the film repeats: the ester group of aconitine
  may have partially hydrolysed over six centuries, obscuring part of the
  signal, and sampling was restricted, so the identification rests on SRS-based
  vibrational evidence. This is a compound identified on an instrument. It is
  not a record of a particular operation.

  ON THE PREPARATION: Ming period texts describe boiling the plant with vinegar
  or combining it with other ingredients including mung beans to weaken its
  effects before use.


ITEM 3 - JAR 1 AT SITE 75, PLAIN OF JARS

  Skopal, N., Pradier, B., Bounxayhip, S., Cooper, C., Dussubieux, L.,
  Devantier-Thomas, T. G., Pilgrim, T., Van Berkel, S., Demko, D.,
  Valentin, F., Skopal, J., Baker, D., Florin, S. A., Posth, C. and
  Clark, G. (2026). The death jar: a new mortuary tradition at the Plain of
  Jars, Lao PDR. Antiquity 100(412), 1050-1067.
  doi.org/10.15184/aqy.2026.10352

  WHAT WAS EXCAVATED: Jar 1 at Site 75, about 70 km north-east of Phonsavan,
  over three field seasons (2022-2024). Base diameter 2.05 m, preserved height
  1.30 m. Inside, a collective assemblage of secondary interments with
  earthenware sherds, an iron knife, a copper-based bell, stone slabs and
  twenty glass beads, twelve of which were analysed for composition and point
  to South Asia, Mesopotamia, Egypt and Southeast Asia.

  ON THE NUMBER, which the film gives in full: the minimum number of
  individuals varies with the element counted - fourteen from left humeri,
  nineteen from right femora and from skulls, and thirty-seven from the
  dentition. Thirty-seven is the highest of those estimates, not a headcount.
  Ages run from about 1.5 years to adulthood. Carious lesions were recorded in
  5.3 per cent of observed teeth (25 of 469).

  ON THE DATING: eight human bone samples were dated directly, three of them
  previously published, giving c. cal AD 890-1160.

  WHAT IS NOT SHOWN: no human remains appear anywhere in this film - no bone,
  no skull, no tooth, no open jar. The count is a drawn graphic. See the
  method note.


ITEM 4 - SEDENTISM WITHOUT FARMING, YUMIN

  Chen, J., Wang, Y., Jia, X., Gao, Y., Zhang, Z., Hu, X., Bao, Q. and
  Chen, F. (2026). Broad-spectrum subsistence facilitated early sedentism in
  the southern Mongolian Plateau revealed by sedimentary ancient DNA.
  Journal of Archaeological Science 193, 106646.
  doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2026.106646

  WHAT WAS MEASURED: sedimentary ancient DNA metagenomics on archaeological
  deposits at the Yumin site (Huade, Ulanqab, Inner Mongolia), dated c.
  8,600-7,400 cal BP. The Yumin culture represents early sedentary occupation
  on the southern plateau, preceding agro-pastoralism.

  WHAT THE FINDING IS - AND IS NOT: the finding is not that people were
  sedentary without agriculture, which was already established here. The
  finding is the subsistence base that made it possible: a broad spectrum of
  resources rather than a single staple. The film says this explicitly, because
  the shorter version is the more exciting one and it is wrong.

  A NOTE ON HOW WE CHECKED IT: this paper was first visible to us only as an
  SSRN entry, that is, as a preprint. Under our own rule a preprint is not
  dispatch material. The Crossref record shows it as a peer-reviewed article in
  the September 2026 issue, so it ran. The SSRN entry was the earlier stage,
  not the final state.


EXCURSUS - THE FOSSIL LEFT ON BEAR ISLAND

  Schoch, R. R., Chakravorti, S., Witzmann, F. and Lindemann, F.-J. (2026).
  A giant plagiosaurid temnospondyl from Bjornoya (Svalbard, Norway) and the
  origin of the plagiosaurid body plan.
  Papers in Palaeontology 12(4), e70115.
  doi.org/10.1002/spp2.70115

  This is palaeontology, not archaeology, and the film says so before it starts.
  It is here because of what the record shows: a British expedition found the
  articulated skeleton on Bjornoya in 1948, could not remove it, took some
  bones and photographs, and left the rest. A Norwegian party rediscovered it
  by chance in 1984 and excavated what the weather had left. The specimen was
  prepared at the State Museum of Natural History Stuttgart and described only
  now, as Arctobatrachus urdensis.

  ON THE SIZE, both figures as published: the abstract states a body length
  "exceeding 2 metres"; press material gives about 2.7 m, an estimate combining
  the skull with the historical photographs. The film carries the conservative
  figure and marks the estimate as an estimate. The skull is roughly 70 cm wide
  and about 21 cm long.

  ON "LARGEST" AND "TOP PREDATOR": both statements are within Plagiosauridae -
  by far the largest known member of that family, and the first read as a top
  predator rather than an ambush feeder. Neither is a claim about Triassic
  animals in general. It is not a giant; it is a giant of its own small family.

  ON THE PHOTOGRAPHS: the 1948 photographs are not shown in this film. They are
  third-party material and copyright applies. What appears on screen is a
  generated reconstruction of an expedition scene and a generic stack of old
  prints - not a copy of any particular historical photograph.
