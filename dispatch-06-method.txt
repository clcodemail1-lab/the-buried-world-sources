THE BURIED WORLD
Dispatch 06 - The Name Was Doing The Work
How this film was made

15 August 2026


THE SELECTION
=============

Three items were chosen from roughly fifty archaeology stories published
between 9 and 15 August 2026. The rule for this channel's Friday strand is
simple and it removes most of them: no peer-reviewed publication, no place in
the film.

It removed the best story of the week again, and for the second fortnight
running it is the same one. New fragments of an imperial decree from
Stratonikeia in western Turkey, issued on 1 August 480, document fifteen years
of tax fraud on an imperial estate and threaten the officials with death. The
material is superb. It was presented at a seminar in Oxford, and the University
of Warsaw states plainly that the full publication is still "a few months"
away. So it stays out of a second film. It fits this film's argument as neatly
as it fitted the last one, which is exactly the kind of reason that must not be
allowed to count.

Also left out, all for the same reason - press announcement, no paper:
rectangular buildings found by survey at Gobekli Tepe (which would have been
the single best case in this film, and which this channel will cover properly
the day it is published), a Bronze Age burial under a Viking ship grave in
Norway, sixteen storage jars in central Turkey, gold bars in a Belgian cellar
wall.

One item was left out for a different reason. A new reading of the Linear A
libation table IO Za 2 from Mount Juktas proposes two divine names behind the
later Greek Dionysos and Demeter. It is deposited on Zenodo, it has not been
peer reviewed, and every step of it rests on the author's own reconstruction of
a script that has not been deciphered. Covering it would have meant spending a
third of a news film assessing one unreviewed proposal. That is media
criticism, and this channel does archaeology.


A REVERSAL, STATED OUT LOUD
===========================

The Phaleron strontium study is in this film. Dispatch 05, published two weeks
ago, deliberately left it out, and said so in this same document: "a mass grave
with iron shackles pulls a film into a register this channel does not work in."

That judgement was about treatment, and it was a fair worry. It is reversed
here because the treatment answers it. This segment is not about how
seventy-nine men died - the film says explicitly that the measurement cannot
tell us that, and it names nobody's guilt. It is about how a piece of iron was
read as a statement of origin for ten years. No human remains are shown at any
point in the film. Nothing in it is staged as violence.

Anyone who thinks the earlier call was the better one is holding a reasonable
position. The channel's practice is to change its mind in public rather than
quietly, so the earlier reasoning stays on the record above.


WHAT WAS ACTUALLY READ
======================

Of the three papers, one could be read in full: the Cemka Hoyuk article in
Antiquity, which is open access under CC-BY. The other two are behind
paywalls, and this film is built on their abstracts, on the institutional
releases from Arizona State University and the University of Cambridge, and on
reporting that quotes the authors directly.

That limitation is visible in the film as a set of absences.

No count is given of how many of the seventy-nine men at Phaleron were
shackled. The reporting offers thirty-six, thirty-nine, "many" and "all"; the
accessible text of the study gives no figure. The narration therefore says
"many still in iron at the wrists" and stops there.

No firing temperature is claimed as measured for individual vessels at Cemka
Hoyuk. The published range of 600 to 700 degrees is stated as the range it is.

No labour figure is extrapolated from the Colombian study to the whole field
system. The paper models a block of five hundred hectares. The film says five
hundred hectares, twice, and names the difference from the five hundred
thousand of the whole complex.


THE FACT CHECK
==============

Every figure was checked before recording. Four things changed as a result, and
all four are places where the reporting went further than the studies.

The first is an age. Secondary coverage puts the Cemka Hoyuk vessels at 11,500
or even 12,000 years old. The publication dates the context to around 9350
calibrated BC - about 11,300 years. The film uses the published date.

The second is a superlative. Several headlines call this the earliest pottery,
full stop. It is not. Fired vessels from East Asian sites are roughly twice
this age. The claim in the paper is regional - south-west Asia - and the film
says so out loud rather than leaving the correction to the comments.

The third is a modal verb. The paper says the fragments "may have been
deliberately fired". Much of the coverage drops the "may". The film keeps it,
and then explains both why the reading is strong (mineral evidence, low-oxygen
conditions, nine separate vessels) and why the hedge is honest (an ordinary
hearth reaches the same temperature).

The fourth is a framing, and it cuts both ways. One outlet ran the Phaleron
study as "archaeologists thought these were foreign invaders - they were
wrong". Another ran it as "study confirms". Neither is right, because there was
no consensus to overturn or confirm. Two families of explanation have stood
side by side since 2016: an internal Athenian killing, with the followers of
Cylon as the usual candidates, or captives from outside. The study decides
between them. The film says that.

One smaller correction, of the kind that matters for anyone following the
citations: at least one report places the Phaleron study in the Journal of
Archaeological Science. It is in Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports, a
different journal from the same publisher. The source list gives the DOI.


WHAT THE METHODS CANNOT DO
==========================

Strontium isotope analysis does not tell you where a person came from. It tells
you which places are incompatible with the chemistry of their childhood, and
several unrelated regions can share a signature. "Compatible with Attica" is
the strongest available statement, and it is the one the film makes. It says
nothing about cause of death, faction, status or year.

The Colombian study is a model, not an excavation. It converts mapped earthwork
volumes into person-days using assumptions about soil, tools and population.
The authors flag their own weakest link themselves: metal tools did not exist
there, so wooden ones are assumed, and wood does not survive in that ground.
They also state that nobody knows why the system was abandoned before the
Spanish arrived. Neither point is smoothed over in the film.

The Cemka Hoyuk material is nine vessels from one site. It shows that fired
containers were made in the region far earlier than the period name implies. It
does not show a tradition, a transmission, or a line of descent to the pottery
of four thousand years later.


IMAGES
======

No photograph in this film is of a real object or a real place. Every image is
either a reconstruction made for this film and marked AI RECONSTRUCTION on
screen, or a diagram drawn for it in code. Nothing is a press picture, and no
third-party image was used as a reference.

Two deliberate omissions. No human remains are depicted anywhere in this film,
and the Phaleron burials are shown as an excavation surface and an iron
shackle, never as bodies. And no image claims to show a specific catalogued
object: the vessels, the shackle and the ridges are reconstructions of a type,
not portraits of a find.

The maps and diagrams are drawn from coordinates rather than generated, because
image models place lettering unreliably and a map that misplaces a river is a
claim the film cannot support.
