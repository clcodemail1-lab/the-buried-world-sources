THE BURIED WORLD
Dispatch 12 - Nobody Writes Down What Everybody Knows
How this film was made, and what the fact check changed


WHY THESE FOUR PAPERS

They share a shape rather than a subject. Each of the four sits at a place where
the written record is unusually rich - Sumerian administrative tablets, the
chronicles of a nomadic court, a Roman town buried mid-sentence, Roman
descriptions of household rite - and each answers a question those texts never
bothered to answer, because everyone alive at the time already knew it. What
people actually ate. Where a mobile camp got its meat. What was burnt under a
bread oven. What went into a censer in a house shrine.

The idea can be said without a technical term in it: nobody writes down what
everybody knows. That constraint is a standing rule for this series, adopted
after the channel judged an earlier dispatch uninteresting because its frame
needed a specialist word to work at all. Nothing in this film's title, opening
or thumbnail asks the viewer to know what a zinc isotope, a nabo or a
morphotype is.

The papers came from the current volumes of the Proceedings of the National
Academy of Sciences, the Journal of Archaeological Science, Antiquity and
Archaeological and Anthropological Sciences. Peer-reviewed publication is a
condition of this series: an excavation announcement without a paper is not
material.


NO FULL TEXTS THIS TIME

Unlike Dispatch 11, this film is built on abstracts and publisher metadata
alone. No paywalled full text was used, and none was needed for anything the
film states - but it sets a hard limit on how far any of the four sections can
go, and that limit is visible in the writing. Where Dispatch 11 could name a
figure from page seven of an article, this one stays inside what the authors
themselves published openly.

Two consequences, both deliberate:

 - The film gives no counts. Not of the olive stones, not of the bones, not of
   the individuals sampled. Those numbers exist; they were not accessible, and
   a number that cannot be checked is worse than no number.
 - The Abu Tbeirah section is the longest of the four, because its abstract is
   the most specific. That is a property of the sources, not a judgement about
   which case matters most.


TWO ERRORS IN OUR OWN NOTES, BOTH FOUND BY THE DOI CHECK

The two Pompeii cases had been sitting in this channel's stock of candidate
topics since Dispatch 09, waiting for a Pompeii episode to clear out of the
schedule. Both entries were wrong, and both errors survived being copied
forward through three later dispatches:

1. The olive stone paper was recorded as article 106659. It is 106648. A source
   list built on the note would have pointed the audience at a different paper
   in the same issue.

2. The incense paper was recorded as being in the Journal of Archaeological
   Science. It is in Antiquity.

Neither was caught by re-reading the note - a note reads the same whether it is
right or wrong. Both were caught by resolving the DOI at Crossref and comparing
the returned record field by field. That check is now done for every citation
in every episode, and it is also why the dates below are given per field.


DATES: THE ISSUE LABEL IS NOT THE DAY

Four papers, four different relationships between "published online", the print
issue label and the day the record was created. The olive stone paper has no
online date at all; its record was created on 18 July 2026 and it carries a
September issue label. The film therefore names the ISSUE for that one and a
month for the other three. It never converts an issue label into a day.

The full table is in dispatch-12-sources.txt.


WHAT WAS CUT, AND WHY

 - "The elite ate differently." Press coverage of the Abu Tbeirah paper says
   so. The paper does not: it studied a NONELITE population and reports that
   diet did not vary by sex within it. An elite comparison would be a claim
   about something the study never measured. This cut also removed the frame
   the episode was originally planned around - "who got what to eat was a
   decision" - because that frame stood on a finding that does not exist. The
   frame was rebuilt; the finding was not invented.

 - Tucume. It was in the stock as a third diet case and could not be verified:
   no publication was findable. Under this series' rule, that ends it.

 - The Latin name of the Roman rite. The rite is in the film, the word is not.
   The frame has to work without a dictionary.

 - Stratonikeia, for the sixth time. The University of Warsaw still says the
   full publication is in preparation. It is checked again at Dispatch 13, and
   if nothing has appeared by then it comes out of the stock rather than being
   passed forward again. A candidate that has been carried for six rounds is
   not a candidate any more.


WHAT THE FILM IS CAREFUL NOT TO SAY

The incense section ends on the authors' own caution, in their terms: no
sediment control samples were preserved from either findspot, so later
contamination cannot be excluded. That caution is theirs, not an objection
raised by this film, and the diagram that carries it says so on the picture.

The grape product is reported as identified, not as certain.

The film does not say what the resin was used for beyond being burnt.


THE PICTURES

Thirteen of the forty-nine shots are diagrams drawn in code from the published
figures. Every other shot is generated, labelled on screen as required, and
depicts no real, identifiable find, site or person. No ancient text is
reproduced anywhere in the film: where writing appears in a diagram it is blank
ruled lines standing for text.

One shot was re-made twice. The cut marks on a bone in the Houmingzi section
first came back with regular incised strokes that read as script - in a film
about what was never written down, the worst possible accident. The fix was not
a better prompt but a different object: broken bone fragments with fractured
ends, where nothing can be mistaken for a sign.
