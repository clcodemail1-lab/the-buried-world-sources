THE BURIED WORLD
Dispatch 17 - Anglo-Saxon Kings Had Silver. They Paid in Copper Anyway

SOURCES AND LIMITS

One publication carries this film, and it was read in full: an open-access
study of the gold and silver finds of ninth-century Northumbria, published in
Antiquity in November 2026. The silver content of the coins is not measured
in it; it comes from the work of Rory Naismith, which the paper cites and the
film names. Two news reports were read for the way the result travelled, and
the film follows the paper where they go further. The companion file
dispatch-17-method.txt holds the complete fact-check table.


THE PUBLICATION

1. Carson, J. L. (2026). Debasement and the production of copper-alloy coins
  in ninth-century Northumbria. Antiquity (First View), pp. 1-16.
  Published online 10 November 2026 (Crossref: published-online and created
  10 November 2026; no volume or issue yet). Open access, CC BY 4.0.
  doi:10.15184/aqy.2026.10454
  -- Source of: the removal of all silver in the 840s and its uniqueness in
  early medieval western Europe; the extent of Northumbria; the raid on
  Lindisfarne in 793 and the fall of the kingdom in 867; the older reading of
  the coins as the sign of a dying kingdom, and its reasons; the design and
  the fiduciary nature of the coins; the special silver and gold pieces of
  Eanred and Archbishop Wigmund; imported tin and zinc; the method (counting
  finds, not weighing them, over half recorded by the Portable Antiquities
  Scheme); the counts of 27 certain and 7 likely silver finds, 23 and 8 with
  silver decoration, 11 and 1 of gold; the Poppleton hoard; the sword pommels
  of Gilling West, York and Ingleton; the comparison with the eighth century,
  with Rome, with Charlemagne and with Byzantium; the motives of profit,
  everyday usefulness and shared identity; and every limit the film states.

2. Naismith, R. (2017, 2023), as cited in (1): the silver content of the coins
  (up to 40 per cent, then at most 10, then none) and the description of the
  styca as the first independently evolved base-metal currency in post-Roman
  western Europe.

HOW THIS FILM WAS MADE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The narration is synthetic speech. Apart from the four credited
photographs, every still is generated; none of the generated images
reproduces a real find, a real site photograph, a figure from the paper or a
real person. Generated coins are deliberately shown worn smooth, so that no
invented inscription appears; every coin you can read in this film is real.
The diagrams are drawn in code.

The opening seconds are built to work without sound - the find first, then a
caption naming the place and the period - and on-screen text is set large
for viewers watching on a television.

The film carries the EU marking for AI-generated content.
