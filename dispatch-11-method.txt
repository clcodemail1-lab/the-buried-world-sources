THE BURIED WORLD
Dispatch 11 - Nothing Of It Was Left
How this film was made, and what the fact check changed


WHY THESE FOUR PAPERS

They share a shape rather than a subject. In each one the object under
discussion no longer exists - a bed, a house, a piece of cloth, a courtly
practice - and it is established anyway. What makes them worth putting side by
side is WHAT the proof hangs on: in all four cases it is a by-product. The
seeds are not the pillow. The mud plaster is not the house. The crushed stone
is not the cloth. The pigeon bones are not the falconry.

The idea can be said in one sentence with no technical term in it: the thing is
gone, the trace is not. That constraint is now a standing rule for this series,
adopted after the channel judged an earlier dispatch uninteresting because its
frame needed a specialist word to work at all.

The papers came from a search of the current volumes of the Journal of
Archaeological Science, its Reports series, Antiquity, Archaeological and
Anthropological Sciences, the Journal of Anthropological Archaeology and the
International Journal of Osteoarchaeology, filtered to items deposited in the
three weeks before the run. Peer-reviewed publication is a condition: an
excavation announcement without a paper is not material for this series.


ONE FULL TEXT, AND WHAT THAT CHANGED

This is the first dispatch in the series built on a full text rather than
abstracts alone. The Czarnowko paper is published open access in Antiquity and
was read in full. The three others - two in Elsevier journals, one at Wiley -
were not reachable: both publishers refuse automated requests, and the same
refusal came back through a browser. For those three, every figure in the
commentary comes from the authors' own abstracts.

The difference is audible. Three things in the Czarnowko section exist nowhere
in that paper's abstract:

 - that none of the twenty-three samples contained moss, although one of the
   deposits had been catalogued for years as a moss pillow;
 - that the cattail fluff itself is essentially absent, because each hair is
   about seventeen microns thick and shatters into single cells, so what
   survives is the seeds that travelled with it;
 - that the impression in one deposit is thirty-two millimetres across and
   matches the foot of a silver cup from the same grave exactly.

The first of those is the hook of the whole film. It came from page seven of a
fifteen-page article. Where the other three cases sound thinner, that is not a
stylistic choice.


SEVEN THINGS THE FACT CHECK CHANGED

1. Buzzards and owls are decoys, not training birds. The Tver abstract lists
   buzzards, owls and corvids as decoy species, and separately names training
   corvids among its diagnostic criteria. A draft had all of them as birds a
   young hawk is flown at in training, which collapses two different jobs into
   one.

2. A description of falconry as practised "on horseback, in company, at
   enormous expense" was cut. None of it is in the source. The commentary now
   uses the paper's own phrase - a prominent emblem of aristocratic status -
   and nothing beyond it. This is the recurring failure mode of this series:
   scenery that sounds like fact.

3. "Poor man's wool" was moved into the present. The full text attributes that
   name to modern sources; a draft implied it was what people called the
   material at the time.

4. The fired-daub claim was tied back to its two conditions. A draft said fire
   turns the mud into something close to low-fired pottery that then lasts
   indefinitely. The abstract says fire AND favourable preservation conditions,
   and says nothing about pottery. The consequence drawn from it - that a house
   which burnt down is better evidence than one that did not - is now clearly
   marked as a consequence rather than as something the paper states.

5. The thread count stopped being vague. A draft called the wool "dense,
   sixteen to eighteen threads to the centimetre". That figure is the warp; the
   weft on the same piece runs twelve. Being vaguer than your source is a form
   of inaccuracy too.

6. "Every ditch and pond in the region" became what the paper actually says
   about where cattail grows: wetlands and the banks of rivers and standing
   water.

7. Castro de Sao Martinho is not called a hillfort. That is a reasonable
   translation of castro and it is not the authors' word, so the film says
   "an Iron Age site" instead.

No publication month appears anywhere in the film. Two of the four records
carry a real online publication date and two carry only a print issue label
that Elsevier assigns months in advance - a distinction that cost the previous
dispatch a re-cut. Rather than explain it in the commentary, the film states no
month at all, and the source list sets out the situation in full.


WHAT THE FILM COULD NOT GET AT

For the three abstract-only cases, anything requiring the full text is absent
rather than estimated: how many daub fragments were analysed at Borsodivanka,
how much crushed dolerite was recovered at Sao Martinho, how many bird bones
the six per cent at Tver represents. Where a number would have needed the body
of the paper, the film says nothing instead of guessing.

Corrections are welcome and will be made out loud.
