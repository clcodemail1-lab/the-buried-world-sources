HOW THIS EPISODE WAS CHECKED

Every figure spoken in this film was traced to a publication, and the date
fields of each DOI were read individually, because a journal's print date is
an issue label and not the day a paper appeared. Nine statements in the first
draft were cut or rewritten during that check; they are described, one by one,
in the methods file that accompanies this list.


THE LIBRARY: THE MANUSCRIPT AND WHAT IT SAYS ABOUT ITSELF

Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, Yale University. MS 408 (the
  "Voynich manuscript"): catalogue record and IIIF manifest 2002046;
  provenance and physical description (pre1600.ms408.HTM).
  https://collections.library.yale.edu/
  -- Source of the shelfmark; of the extent "ff. 102 +1" (the catalogue's own
  count, used instead of the often-quoted "about 240 pages"); of the six
  sections; and of the provenance, including the Jesuit college at Frascati
  and the purchase by Wilfrid Voynich in 1912. Source of every page image of
  the manuscript shown in this film.

Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library. Highlights page for MS 408.
  -- Used for: the list of sections and the sequence of owners after Voynich,
  including the purchase by H. P. Kraus in 1961 and the gift to Yale in 1969.

Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library. Transcription of the letter of Johannes
  Marcus Marci to Athanasius Kircher, 1665/1666 (pre1600.ms408.HTM).
  -- Used for: the letter that sent the book to Kircher, the report of a price
  of 600 ducats given as hearsay ("retulit mihi D. Doctor Raphael"), and the
  attribution to Roger Bacon, likewise reported at second hand.


THE MATERIAL EVIDENCE

McCrone Associates (2009). Report to the Beinecke Library on the pigments and
  ink of MS 408, 1 April 2009 (PDF hosted by library.yale.edu).
  -- Used for: the finding of iron gall ink; azurite; a copper-based green; a
  red ochre; nothing out of its time; and the ultraviolet examination of the
  signature on f1r.

University of Arizona (2011). Announcement of the radiocarbon dating of four
  samples from four leaves of MS 408 (dated 2009, announced February 2011),
  as reported by LiveScience and medievalists.net, 11 February 2011.
  -- Used for: the range 1404-1438, given without a confidence figure because
  the underlying report is not public.

Yale News (31 October 2016). News item on the dating of MS 408.
  -- Used for: the radiocarbon range and the account of the dating.

Clemens, R. (ed.) (2016). The Voynich Manuscript. New Haven: Yale University
  Press. ISBN 9780300217230.
  -- Used for: the chapter on the physical findings (radiocarbon, pigments).

Davis, L. F. (2024). Multispectral Imaging and the Voynich Manuscript.
  Manuscript Road Trip, 8 September 2024.
  https://manuscriptroadtrip.wordpress.com/2024/09/08/multispectral-imaging-and-the-voynich-manuscript/
  -- Used for: the signature of Jacobus Horcicky de Tepenecz on f1r and the
  three alphabet columns on f1r, which the author describes as not being a key.


THE SCRIBES, THE TEXT AND ITS STATISTICS

Davis, L. F. (2020). How Many Glyphs and How Many Scribes? Digital
  Paleography and the Voynich Manuscript. Manuscript Studies 5 (1), 164-180.
  DOI 10.1353/mns.2020.0011
  -- Used for: the number of hands (at least four, more likely five) and the
  discussion of how many glyphs the script has. The abstract is paywalled;
  the wording follows the review by Bowern and Lindemann.

Bowern, C. and Lindemann, L. (2021). The Linguistics of the Voynich
  Manuscript. Annual Review of Linguistics 7, 285-308.
  DOI 10.1146/annurev-linguistics-011619-030613
  -- Used for: Zipf's law in both dialects; near-copies of words differing by
  one sign; the line as a unit; conditional entropy lower than in any of the
  250 languages compared; the rate of immediate word repetition, which is
  within the natural-language range; Currier's two dialects; the month names
  in a form of French or Occitan; the lines in Latin script on the last page;
  and the statement that only two lines of the 2017 reading were published.

Montemurro, M. A. and Zanette, D. H. (2013). Keywords and Co-Occurrence
  Patterns in the Voynich Manuscript: An Information-Theoretic Analysis.
  PLOS ONE 8 (6), e66344. Published online 21 June 2013.
  DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0066344
  -- Used for: keywords clustering by section, with the strongest link between
  the herbal and the pharmaceutical sections.

Currier, P. (1976). Contribution to the Washington seminar papers on the
  Voynich manuscript, edited by M. E. D'Imperio.
  -- Used for: the division of the text into two dialects, A and B (cited via
  Bowern and Lindemann 2021, which was read in full).

D'Imperio, M. E. (1978). The Voynich Manuscript: An Elegant Enigma.
  Fort Meade: National Security Agency / Central Security Service.
  -- Used for: the account of the Friedman group's work of 1944-46 and its
  conclusion that the text is an artificial language; the survey itself.


THE READINGS

Manly, J. M. (1931). Speculum 6 (3), 345-391. DOI 10.2307/2848508
  -- Used for: the refutation of the 1921 reading (microscopic shorthand, Roger
  Bacon, a microscope), which Manly showed to rest on cracked ink.

Bax, S. (2014). A proposed partial decoding of the Voynich script.
  January 2014 (preprint).
  -- Used for: the 2014 reading of plant names and a star, described by its
  author as provisional and partial. The number of words is not given in the
  film because the sources disagree (five or ten).

Times Literary Supplement (September 2017). Article presenting a reading of
  the manuscript as abbreviated Latin on women's health.
  -- Paywalled; title and date only. Used for: the existence and the year of
  the claim. Two lines were published (Bowern and Lindemann 2021).

Cheshire, G. (2019). The Language and Writing System of MS408 (Voynich)
  Explained. Romance Studies 37 (1), 30-67. Online 29 April 2019.
  DOI 10.1080/02639904.2019.1599566
  -- Used for: the 2019 reading as proto-Romance, compiled by nuns for a queen
  of Aragon.

University of Bristol (May 2019). Statement on the Voynich manuscript.
  https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2019/may/voynich-manuscript.html
  -- Used for: the announcement and its withdrawal within days, quoted in the
  statement's own words.

YouTube video "Wrongly Assumed" (uploaded 20 September 2026).

THE TEST: HOW A DECIPHERMENT IS CHECKED

Ventris, M. and Chadwick, J. (1953). Evidence for Greek Dialect in the
  Mycenaean Archives. Journal of Hellenic Studies 73, 84-103.
  DOI 10.2307/628239
  -- Used for: the decipherment of Linear B, announced in 1952-53, and the
  Pylos tablet that tested it.


THE OTHER POSSIBILITY

Rugg, G. (2004). An Elegant Hoax? A Possible Solution to the Voynich
  Manuscript. Cryptologia 28 (1), 31-46. DOI 10.1080/0161-110491892755
  -- Used for: the syllable table and the grille that reproduce the
  statistics; the grille described in 1550.

Rugg, G. and Taylor, G. (2016). Hoaxing Statistical Features of the Voynich
  Manuscript. Cryptologia 41 (3).
  DOI 10.1080/01611194.2016.1206753
  -- Used for: the follow-up work on the 2004 proposal.

Timm, T. and Schinner, A. (2020). A Possible Generating Algorithm of the
  Voynich Manuscript. Cryptologia 44 (1), 1-19. Online 25 May 2019.
  DOI 10.1080/01611194.2019.1596999
  -- Used for: the self-citation method (copy the previous word, change one

WHERE THIS EPISODE DIFFERS FROM THE USUAL ACCOUNT

- It gives the library's own count, ff. 102 +1, and not "about 240 pages".
- It gives the radiocarbon range without a confidence figure.
- It does not say that a Prague nobleman or the emperor bought the book. The
  price of 600 ducats is hearsay in the Marci letter; what is documented is a
  signature on f1r.
- It does not say that the book is a hoax, a language or a cipher, and it does
  not say that any of the five readings has been proved wrong. It says that none
  has been used on the next page by somebody else.
- It does not say that words repeat more often than in any language. The
  review's finding concerns immediate repetition, which is within the range of
  natural language.


WHAT THE FILM DOES NOT CLAIM

- It does not claim that the Beinecke "released" the scans. The library put them
  online for study; the legal basis for using them is stated in the methods
  file.
- It does not name the researchers behind the readings of 2014, 2017 and 2019 in
  the narration, because the argument is about a pattern and not about a
  person. They are named in this list.
- It does not claim that the manuscript is, or is not, a language.

WHAT THE IMAGES ARE

TWENTY-FOUR of the 56 shots show the manuscript itself. They are not generated.
They are the Beinecke Library's own scans of MS 408, taken from its IIIF image
service, shown whole or cropped and otherwise unaltered.
Credit: Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, Yale University.
The legal basis is stated in the methods file: the book is an anonymous work of
the fifteenth century in the public domain, section 68 of the German Copyright
Act removes any related right in the reproduction, and the library's own page
states that Yale University claims no rights in public domain works.

The other TWENTY photograph-like shots - offices, a university building, a
corridor, a reading room, laboratories, a bookshop, a letter, and four painted
historical scenes - are generated, and are marked as such on screen with the EU
marking in the first fifteen seconds and on every such shot. No generated image
shows a page of the manuscript, and no generated image contains writing of any
kind: in a film about a script nobody can read, invented script would be a
falsehood. No generated image shows the face of any real person.

The TWELVE diagrams are drawn in code. Where they show measurements that were
not made for this film - the word-frequency curve, the entropy bars, the
distribution of the two dialects - they say "schematic" in their own footers.
Where three possibilities are set side by side, each gets the same area, the
same type size and the same colour, because the film does not decide between
them and the graphics are not allowed to decide either.

Corrections are welcome and will be made out loud.
